Accounts, calibration and scope

What the model does not constrain

Two limitations are inherent to the formulation rather than to the data, and both bear on how results should be read.

Processing location does not respond smoothly. Processes have constant returns to scale and a constant value-added cost, so a process has no supply response of its own: below a threshold set by freight it does not move at all, and above it the whole industry moves at once. Why processes, and not supply and demand per transformation measures this and explains where it bites — chiefly on products left homogeneous, since an Armington specification on the output restores a smooth response.

Otherwise the remedy is to say what capacity exists. A Limit on the activity of a process bounds it, region by region if need be, and a binding bound earns a quasi-rent reported in Results.activity and Results.limits — what another unit of plant capacity would be worth there. Without such a bound, and without Armington on its output, read the geography of processing far more cautiously than that of prices or of final demand.

Prices come from a continuous relaxation. All quantities are continuous, so the model knows nothing of indivisible investments, minimum efficient scale or shutdown decisions.

Linear constraints on any of the model's quantities — a resource shared by several products, a quota, a minimum share — can be added with @limit without leaving the formulation: they keep the problem convex, and each comes back with its rent; see Limits on any quantity.

Physical accounts

Every quantity the model solves for is physical — what each region produced, what it shipped to every other, what its households consumed, what its plants took in — and every balance between them is an equality. Two kinds of quantity are not sums, though, but CES aggregates:

  • the composite a region uses of an Armington product, made of the varieties of every origin;
  • the bundle a process uses from a ces nest, made of several inputs.

A CES aggregate equals the sum of its members only at the mix it was calibrated at. Away from it, a mixed bundle counts for more, or less, than the physical quantity in it: a region's households and plants can draw a composite of 100 out of 104 m³ delivered, or a mill can turn 2.47 m³ of wood into a tonne of pellets where its recipe says 2.39. That is not an error of the solver but what a CES is — a production or preference function, with a gain from mixing built in — and it is the price of the smooth, scale-free substitution it gives.

The results therefore keep two accounts side by side:

  • in composite units, the quantities the demand curves, the recipes and the prices refer to. This account is exact in value: the composite times the price its users pay is what was spent on the varieties, delivered.
  • in physical units, the quantities that moved. The varieties a region received are its received, in Results.composites, and every use of the region's composite — final demand and each plant — is credited with the same physical content per unit, the ratio received / composite. That assumes every user in a region gets the same mix of origins, the one assumption the account makes. Final consumption has a physical column, process inputs are in Results.inputs, and Results.recipes sets the physical inputs of each nest against what its recipe requires: the gap is the quantity the aggregation used up or made up.

With them the physical account closes: production equals shipments, shipments equal receipts, receipts equal the physical uses, and a plant's physical inputs equal its recipe plus the gap. In the forest examples the gaps come to a few hundredths of a per cent of the world's wood, and up to a few per cent for one product in one region under a large shock — small, but worth reporting wherever the model feeds a physical balance, such as carbon or resource use.

What it would take to make the composites add up

Where a physical identity has to hold exactly, there are exact aggregators: sigma = 0 (fixed proportions) and sigma = Inf (perfect substitutes, a plain sum), and, for inputs, a choice between alternative fixed recipes written as separate processes. Keeping imperfect substitution and an aggregate that is a plain sum is possible too, but each way of doing it gives up something else, and there are three properties to choose from:

one convex program, prices as dualssubstitution depends on price ratios onlythe aggregate is a plain sum
CES, as here✓✓
logit shares, with a variety term in the objective✓✓
additive CES (van der Mensbrugghe & Peters, 2016)✓✓

The logit keeps the program and the plain sum, but its shares respond to price differences: doubling every price moves them, and users pay more per unit of the aggregate than was spent per unit. The additive CES keeps the plain sum and the ratios — its shares follow the usual CES rule, and the aggregate is priced at the average unit value — but it chooses its mix as if minimising the cost of a CES aggregate while counting the result as a sum, which no objective in the quantities alone describes; it belongs in an equilibrium model posed as a complementarity problem, the paradigm this package does not take. Its results also depend on how base-year values are split into prices and volumes, which those of the CES do not. Van der Mensbrugghe & Peters find the deviations of the standard form, for land in a global model, of 3.5% in the world and over 8% in some countries — the same order as here.

Material accounts

What the goods are made of — carbon, dry matter, a metal — can be traced through the same solution with material_accounts, following Lorang & Gerlagh (2026). A Material enters with primary supply at a given content per unit; it travels with the goods, each region's production carrying the content of what made it and its users the average content of what they received; and in a process it is either lost — burnt, emitted, discarded, at a given share — or carried into the outputs. How much of it a unit of a manufactured good carries is therefore not given but follows from the solution: a mill that changes its mix changes the content of what it makes, and the gap of the physical account becomes, in these terms, a change in the material intensity of the output rather than a quantity left unexplained.

These accounts close exactly — every region's material balance, and the world's: what was extracted was consumed or lost — and the consumed column is each region's footprint, the material embodied in its final consumption. They describe the solution and do not change it, as long as nothing in the model depends on the content of the goods. A tax per tonne of embodied material, or a cap on it, would make the intensities part of the equilibrium; that is not implemented.

Calibration

A reasonable first reaction to all of this is: if I already know what was produced last year, and at what price, what is the model for?

The answer is that an observation is a point, and a point with an elasticity attached is a curve. The model never tells you the base year — you measured it. It tells you where the curves' intersection moves when something changes, and it does so for every product, region and process at once, with material balance, zero profit and no-arbitrage all holding simultaneously. That joint consistency is the thing no observation can give you, because it is a property of the system rather than of any one market.

A consistent benchmark is a fixed point

There is a sharp way to see what calibration does. If the data you anchor on are an equilibrium, the model reproduces them exactly and adds nothing:

A  steel  price 182.3283 → 182.3283    relative difference 2.5e-13
B  steel  price 190.3283 → 190.3283    relative difference 1.3e-13
A  ore    price  63.9552 →  63.9552    relative difference 5.1e-13

That is benchmark replication, and reproducing it is the standard test that the data and the structure agree with each other.

Real data are never an equilibrium — they carry measurement error, aggregation over products and periods, margins the model does not represent, and markets that were not clearing. Anchor one price 5% away from its consistent value and the model refuses to return it:

A  steel  anchored at 191.445   model returns  183.64   gap 4.1%
A  ore    anchored at  63.955   model returns   64.77   gap 1.3%

It splits the difference, because your numbers cannot all be true at once. That gap is not a nuisance: it is the model telling you so, and its size is a measure of how far your data are from being a coherent description of a market.

What is actually calibrated

Entering p0 and q0 is not calibration, it is data entry. Calibration is choosing the parameters you do not observe so that the benchmark becomes an equilibrium:

  • vacost of each process in each region, from the observed output prices less the observed cost of its inputs there — which makes zero profit hold exactly at base-year prices;
  • transport_costs on each route, from the observed price gap between its ends on routes that were actually used;
  • armington_calibration for the trade shares and the scale of each composite, from the base-year bilateral matrix at base-year delivered prices, through every level of the origin groups — not from the raw observed shares, for the reason given under Imperfect substitution between origins (Armington); armington_shares does the shares alone for a flat CES;
  • ces_calibration for the shares of any other CES group — the substitutes in a DemandNest, the inputs of a ces nest — and for the p0 and q0 of a demand curve over a bundle, which are its CES price index and the base-year spending divided by that index;
  • shift on the curves, to absorb what remains.

The forest examples do all of it from base-year data read from files: from partial, rounded statistics, where the model lands near the data rather than on them, and from complete, consistent data, where it returns them exactly.

Calibrating with partial data

Data will be missing, and what is there will not quite agree. Neither stops a calibration, and neither matters as much as it seems, because scenario results are differences from the model's own reference run, never from the data. The question is not whether the reference run reproduces every observed number, but whether it is right about what the differences depend on.

That depends on the mechanics of the model:

  • Constant elasticities give the same percentage response wherever the reference run sits. A curve shifted by 10% moves its price by the same percentage whether the price is 90 or 110, so much of a scenario is robust to a reference run that sits some way from the data.
  • Nothing protects a scenario from a reference run that is wrong about what the shock runs into. A capacity or a logistic limit that the reference run places nearer to binding than it was binds sooner, and changes the result in kind, not only in size. Market shares matter the same way — how strongly one region's price passes into another's is $\sigma$ times the share — and so do relative sizes, the margins of the processes, and where they run.
  • The detail of flows inside a group of regions matters little, as long as the totals around it are right: the group's trade with the outside, and each member's production and use.

The forest examples measure this on one economy and two scenarios. A reference run calibrated on partial data — every flow touching the regions of one country imputed from national totals — gives practically the results of one calibrated on complete data. One left uncalibrated, with a single price per product, trade shares from a rule and guessed costs, gets most of the results about right and doubles the main one, because it places a logistic limit closer to binding than the data do.

That says where to spend the effort:

get rightcan be imputed or approximated
production and use by regionflows between regions of one country — impute_flows
trade at the level customs report itregional prices: the national price, or it adjusted by transport
a price level for each product, in each region where it differsthe recipes of the processes: published conversion factors, one region's input mix
the constraints your scenarios will run into: capacities, logistic limits, policiesconsumer prices: derived from flows and producer prices

impute_flows fills in a trade matrix with the flows closest to a gravity pattern that meet every known total. It works best on the part that is actually unknown. When customs report a country's trade with each partner but not which of its regions it went through, impute only the country's corner of the matrix: its regions with their production and use, and each partner standing for its trade with the country as a whole — what it sent in, as an origin, and what it took out, as a destination:

## for one product; `country` are the regions of the country, `partners` the rest
corner = impute_flows(
    production = merge(Dict(r => production[r] for r in country),
                       Dict(b => imports_from[b] for b in partners)),
    use        = merge(Dict(r => use[r] for r in country),
                       Dict(b => exports_to[b] for b in partners)),
    distance = distance, decay = 1.4, home_bias = 3.0,
    totals = Dict((partners, partners) => 0.0))   # their trade with each other is known

The rest of the matrix is what customs report between partners, and what each partner keeps of its own production; the whole goes to armington_calibration as if it had been observed. The second forest page does exactly this.

Inconsistent data need no special treatment: the reference run is their reconciliation, the one point where every balance holds, and its distance from the data says where the data disagree. Only two things are hard errors: a region exporting more than it produced, which leaves its own deliveries negative, and a process with a negative margin, which the model would close.

Never re-anchor on your own output

In a multi-period run the temptation is to re-base each period on the last: set q0 and p0 to the quantities and prices the model just produced, and carry on. Do not. Written out, that makes demand

\[D_t = q_{t-1}\left(\frac{P_t}{P_{t-1}}\right)^{-\eta}\]

which is precisely the lagged-ratio form this package avoids, arrived at from another direction. Its results depend on the path taken rather than the state reached, its errors compound, and the elasticity you estimated is no longer anchored at the level you estimated it for.

p0 and q0 stay at the calibration point for the whole run. Everything that moves between periods moves through shift and the limits, computed from state and from exogenous drivers. The discipline in one sentence:

Use model output to update physical state, never behavioural parameters.

Updating a fish stock by stock + growth − catch is an accounting identity, and the catch may perfectly well come from the model. Updating q0 to last period's quantity is a silent re-estimation of behaviour from your own output, and is a different thing entirely.

The one re-anchoring that is legitimate is to new data: if observations for a later year arrive and you want to forecast from there, that is a fresh calibration against reality.

Dynamics are somebody else's job

This package solves one equilibrium, at one point in time, and that is the whole of its remit. There is no time index anywhere in it and there will not be: a model that needs several periods runs solve_market once per period and carries the state between them itself. Keeping the boundary there is what lets the same engine sit under a fishery model with annual steps, a capacity-planning study with five-year ones, and a one-shot policy comparison with no time at all.

What follows is therefore advice to whoever writes that loop, not a description of something Markets.jl does.

The approach worth taking is recursive dynamics: solve a full static equilibrium each period and link periods only through physical state, updated from the previous period's solution:

  • resource stocks and their growth shift the primary supply curves;
  • installed capacity bounds process activity;
  • exogenous drivers (income, population, ...) shift the demand curves.

Demand and supply remain level functions of price in every period, e.g. $D_t(P) = \alpha_t P^{-\eta}$, with the shifters of Everything other than the price carrying $\alpha_t$. That is the intended seam: the loop updates shift and the limits between periods and never touches the fitted curves.

This deliberately avoids the form used in some recursive models, where demand in a period is a function of demand and price in the previous period, $D_t / D_{t-1} = (P_t / P_{t-1})^{\eta}$. That form is a finite-difference proxy for an elasticity, so its results depend on the path taken and its errors compound across periods. (The ratio form is at least dimensionless, so the objection is about path dependence, not about units.) Anchoring each period on a static equilibrium with level functions keeps the dynamics interpretable.

Planned extensions

These are things the engine itself might grow. A time loop is not among them, for the reason given under Dynamics are somebody else's job.

  • Armington refinements: a separate composite per user (final demand and each process) instead of one per region, as GTAP does for the domestic-versus-imported margin; and a CET nest on the export side, so that redirecting output between destinations is costly for producers too, which is the mirror image of the import-side nest built here.
  • Ad valorem taxes without iteration, which would need the model restated as a complementarity problem rather than an optimisation.
  • Estimation of the parameters from data.
