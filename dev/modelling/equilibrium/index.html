Equilibrium, curves and welfare

Partial, not general, equilibrium

The model represents one sector in detail (its products, processes and trade) and treats the rest of the economy as exogenous: income, population or construction activity enter only through the position of the demand curves, and the prices of non-modelled inputs (labour, energy, ...) enter only through the value-added cost of each process. A general-equilibrium model would add the accounting of the whole economy without adding much detail to the markets of the sector.

The equilibrium as surplus maximisation

Under perfect competition, the competitive spatial equilibrium is the solution of a single optimisation that maximises net social surplus. The problem was posed by Enke (1951) — who solved it by building an electrical circuit — turned into an optimisation by Samuelson (1952), and given the programming formulation everything since has used, by Takayama & Judge (1964). The same formulation, applied to the global forest sector, is what [C3] solves:

\[\max \; \underbrace{\sum \int_0^{D} P^D(x)\,dx}_{\text{gross consumer benefit}} \;-\; \underbrace{\sum \int_0^{S} P^S(x)\,dx}_{\text{primary resource cost}} \;-\; \underbrace{\sum c_k z_k}_{\text{value-added cost}} \;-\; \underbrace{\sum \tau\, T}_{\text{transport cost}}\]

subject to a material balance for every product in every region. This is equivalent to "clearing the markets": the first-order conditions of the optimisation are the market-clearing conditions. One solve gives everything:

  • the quantities (production, consumption, trade, process activity) are the primal solution;
  • the prices are the dual variables of the material balances: the value of one more unit of a product available in a region.

There is no need to iterate on prices or quantities to clear markets.

Where is the producer surplus?

The objective contains the producer surplus even though it is not written as a separate term. Let $\pi$ be the equilibrium prices. Adding and subtracting the values $\pi D$ and $\pi S$ splits net social surplus into:

  • consumer surplus, $\int_0^D P^D - \pi D$, the area between the demand curve and the price;
  • primary producer surplus, $\pi S - \int_0^S P^S$, the area between the price and the supply curve (the rent of the resource owners, e.g. mine or land owners);
  • processors' profit, value of outputs − value of inputs − value-added cost;
  • traders' profit, $(\pi_{to} - \pi_{from} - \tau)\,T$ on each route.

The material balances make all the $\pi$ terms cancel, so the sum of the four is exactly the objective. Processes have constant returns to scale and trade is competitive, so at the equilibrium the last two are zero: an active process earns exactly its costs, and goods are shipped only where the price gap equals the transport cost. All producer surplus is therefore the rent of primary supply, which is in the objective as the area above the supply curves.

Demand and supply curves

Final demand and primary supply have constant elasticity, calibrated to pass through a reference point $(q_0, p_0)$:

\[P^D(D) = p_0 \left(\frac{D}{q_0}\right)^{-1/\eta}, \qquad P^S(S) = p_0 \left(\frac{S}{q_0}\right)^{1/\varepsilon}\]

Both elasticities need only be positive. Demand may be inelastic, which matters because that is how demand for most fuels, foods and basic materials is estimated — for forest products, for example, see Buongiorno (2015) for the orders of magnitude: earlier versions of this package required $\eta > 1$, on the grounds that for $\eta \le 1$ the area under the demand curve from zero is infinite. That argument does not hold. The objective needs an antiderivative of the inverse demand, not the integral from zero, and the additive constant — infinite though it is — changes neither the optimum nor the first-order conditions, hence neither quantities nor prices:

\[\int^D P^D = \frac{a}{1-1/\eta}\, D^{\,1-1/\eta} \quad (\eta \ne 1), \qquad \int^D P^D = a \log D \quad (\eta = 1)\]

With $\eta < 1$ the coefficient and the exponent are both negative, so the term is still increasing and concave and the program is still convex. What is lost is the level of welfare, and with it some of the comparisons between scenarios: see Welfare when demand is inelastic.

Warning

Inelastic demand changes results substantially, and in the direction people often forget: with $\eta$ well below 1 a supply shock is absorbed by prices rather than by quantities. Using $\eta > 1$ for a product whose demand is really inelastic makes a model systematically under-react in price and over-react in volume.

A demand curve on its own depends on its own price alone. Products that compete for the same use are instead grouped under one curve, which is what gives cross-price effects between them — see Substitution in final demand. Either way the demand system is the gradient of the objective's benefit term, and that integrability is what makes the surplus-maximisation equivalence legitimate in the first place; it is also why the cross-price structure has to be a tree rather than a free matrix.

Straight lines

A curve can instead be a straight line, $D = \alpha + \beta p$ with $\beta < 0$ or $S = \alpha + \beta p$ with $\beta > 0$: the form of the classic spatial-equilibrium models from Samuelson (1952) and Takayama and Judge onwards, and of most mathematical-programming sector models. Its integral is quadratic, so the objective stays concave and the program convex.

The two forms agree at the reference point and in the slope there, so small changes are the same in both. They part on large ones. A line has a finite choke price, $-\alpha/\beta$, above which nothing is demanded; a constant-elasticity demand never reaches zero. And the elasticity of a line grows as the price rises: a demand that is inelastic at the reference point becomes elastic further up. A large supply shock therefore raises the price less along a line than along a constant-elasticity curve calibrated to the same point. Neither is more correct; the line is what a linear regression estimates, and what a model built on one should be ported with.

A line through a reference point is the tangent there to the constant-elasticity curve, form = :linear in DemandCurve and SupplyCurve. The flat curve, elasticity = Inf, is the same in both forms: a price given from outside, as world prices are for a small country.

Everything other than the price

A curve says how much is offered or wanted at a given price. Its position depends on everything else: how much ore is in the ground or how many fish are in the sea, how good the roads and ports are, how many workers and machines there are, how many people live in the region and what they earn. Those are exogenous to a partial-equilibrium model and have to enter it somehow. There are three standard devices, and they are not interchangeable.

A multiplicative shifter — the workhorse

Scale the whole curve by a factor built from the drivers and the elasticity of the curve with respect to each:

\[S(P) = \underbrace{\prod_j \left(\frac{Z_j}{Z_j^0}\right)^{\gamma_j}}_{\text{shift}} \; q_0 \left(\frac{P}{p_0}\right)^{\varepsilon}\]

This is what SupplyCurve's and DemandCurve's shift field holds, and what exogenous_shift builds. It is the device most sector partial-equilibrium models use to link a resource to what is taken from it — the forest to the harvest, the fish stock to the catch — and there are three good reasons for that.

It comes free with the estimation. The regression that gives the price elasticity — $\log S$ on $\log P$ — gives the $\gamma_j$ in the same step if the drivers are in it. No separate exercise is needed.

It is safe: a positive factor times a positive curve stays positive, at any level of any driver. An additive shifter, $S = q_0 (P/p_0)^\varepsilon + A$, can drive supply negative and destroys the constant-elasticity reading of $\varepsilon$. Use addition only where something genuinely arrives in a fixed quantity regardless of price — a stockpile released by a government, scrap collected under a recycling mandate, a quota of imports from outside the model. In this package that is a separate SupplyCurve held to that quantity by a Limit, not a shifter.

It separates calibration from scenario. p0 and q0 keep holding the base-year point the curve was fitted to; the scenario lives in shift. Rewrite q0 instead and the calibration is gone, which matters most in a recursive-dynamic run, where the time loop updates the shifter from the state of the resource each period and must not touch the fitted curve.

Quantity shifters and cost shifters are the same thing here

A new road to a mine does not create ore, it lowers the cost of getting it out — a cost shifter, not a quantity one. With an isoelastic curve the distinction collapses: multiplying the quantity by $m$ is exactly multiplying the marginal cost by $m^{-1/\varepsilon}$, since $b = p_0 (m q_0)^{-1/\varepsilon}$. With $\varepsilon = 0.5$, offering 10% more ore at every price is the same as mining it 17% more cheaply at every volume. What matters is only that the elasticity you apply is the one your $\gamma$ was estimated against.

On a straight line the two part. Multiplying the quantity rotates the line about the price at which supply starts, $-\alpha/\beta$, which stays where it was; making every unit cheaper would lower that price too. A driver that works through cost belongs in $\alpha$ on a line, which is also where a regression in levels puts it.

A ceiling — for a limit that binds

A shifter scales a curve; it never stops it. When the constraint is a genuine limit — a fishing quota, a mining licence, the capacity of the rigs, boats and crews that exist this year — the right device is a bound:

\[S \le \bar S(Z)\]

which is a Limit on the supply:

@limit "cod quota" supply(region = "EU", product = "cod") <= 350

The difference is not cosmetic. At a binding ceiling the price parts company with the marginal cost on the curve, and the gap is the scarcity rent of the resource — the economically interesting quantity, and the one a pure shifter cannot produce. Doubling a shifter doubles supply at every price; a ceiling says that beyond a point no price will buy more.

The rent is the price producers get minus what the curve says the last unit cost, taxes on production included:

rent = producer_price - ((1 + t_rel) * Markets.curve_price(s, S) + t_abs)

which is the multiplier of the ceiling, and is how Results.supply and Results.limits report it: zero whenever the ceiling does not bind.

A ceiling set above what would have been produced anyway changes nothing, so it is safe to carry one on every curve that has a known physical limit.

A ceiling can also be soft: crossed at a given cost per unit, which caps its rent at that cost. That is a device for holding a quantity near a reference — trade near its recent levels, in the tradition of the recursive forest sector models — rather than a physical limit, and its cost is not part of welfare. See Soft limits.

A structural supply — when the factors are the point

The two devices above are reduced-form: the curve is estimated, and the drivers move it. The alternative is to derive supply from the technology instead, by making the resource a product and its extraction a process. In a fishery model, for example:

  • the fish stock becomes a product with its own curve or endowment, bounded by a Limit;
  • fishing becomes a Process that consumes the stock, fuel and whatever else the model prices, and yields landed fish;
  • labour, boats and access then enter as priced inputs or as bounds, and the shape of the resulting supply curve is derived rather than assumed.

This is how a computable general equilibrium model treats factors of production, and it is the honest route when the question is about the factors themselves — the effect of a labour shortage, of a fuel subsidy, of a fleet buy-back. The price to pay is the data: factor shares and factor prices by region, which a reduced-form shifter does not need.

The forest example takes the reduced-form route instead: its roundwood comes from supply curves, and its storm scenario moves one of them through its shift rather than modelling the forest itself.

Welfare when demand is inelastic

Results.welfare is net social surplus at the solution: the terms of the objective evaluated with every tax left out, a tax being a transfer, and without the penalties of soft limits. The objective itself is not welfare as soon as there is a tax or a soft limit. Whether the level of welfare means anything, and which differences between scenarios do, depends on the demand curves.

When every demand curve has $\eta > 1$ the antiderivative in the objective is zero at zero, so it is the area under the curve from zero. Welfare is then a true level, and two scenarios can be compared whatever differs between them. A straight line or a flat curve counts as such a curve: the area under it from zero is finite.

When at least one demand curve has $\eta \le 1$ the area under it from zero is infinite: as the quantity falls towards zero the price rises fast enough that the area never stops growing. The model keeps the antiderivative of Demand and supply curves and leaves out the infinite remainder, so

\[W_{\text{true}} = K + W_{\text{reported}}\]

where $K$ is infinite and depends on the curve: its elasticity, its reference point and its shifter. Three things follow.

The level means nothing

Neither the size nor the sign of welfare or objective says anything on its own. The forest example, whose demand elasticities lie between 0.3 and 0.45, reports a negative objective, and that is not a market destroying value.

Differences are exact when the inelastic curves are the same

Between two runs whose inelastic demand curves are identical, $K$ is the same in both and cancels:

\[(K + W_2) - (K + W_1) = W_2 - W_1\]

The difference is then the exact change in consumer surplus, producer surplus and tax revenue together, in money: its size as well as its sign. When an inelastic curve itself differs, the two runs leave out different infinite amounts, the difference between their welfare is meaningless, and a different but equally valid antiderivative could even reverse its sign:

What differs between the two runsDifference in welfarePrices, quantities, trade, activity, rents
supply curves, their shifters included; value-added and transport costs; limits; taxes; demand curves with $\eta > 1$exact, in size and signexact
on a demand curve with $\eta \le 1$: its elasticity, its shifter, its reference point (p0, q0) or its formmeaningless, in size and in signexact

The last column holds in every case. Prices and quantities come from the first-order conditions of the program, which $K$ does not enter, so a projection that moves the demand curves with their shifters is exact in everything it reports except welfare.

Only differences, not ratios

A difference removes $K$; a ratio keeps it. The relative change $(W_2 - W_1) / (K + W_1)$ has a numerator that is right and a denominator that depends on where the zero was put. A fall in reported welfare from −1000 to −1020 is a loss of 20 whatever $K$ is, but as a percentage:

Where the zero is putBeforeAfterChange
as reported, $K = 0$−1000−1020+2%, a "rise", the base being negative
$K = 5\,000$40003980−0.5%
$K = 1\,000\,000$999,000998,980−0.002%

Every line describes the same loss, and none is more correct than another: with the true, infinite $K$ every change is 0%. It is the difference between Celsius and Kelvin, the same degree with the zero elsewhere: warming from 10 °C to 20 °C is +10 degrees on both scales, but +100% in Celsius and +3.5% in Kelvin. To express a welfare change in relative terms, divide it by something that has a true zero: the value of the market, $\pi D$, producer surplus, or the revenue a tax raises.

When the inelastic curves differ

When the scenarios move an inelastic demand curve, the gap in consumer surplus between the two curves is itself infinite — a demand shifted outwards lies above the old one at every quantity — so no figure the model could report would compare them. What remains comparable is:

  • producer surplus, $\pi S - \int_0^S P^S$, since the area under a supply curve is finite at every elasticity;

  • consumer surplus over a common range of prices: the area under the demand curve between the price $p$ consumers pay and a ceiling $\bar p$ chosen once for all the scenarios. For a curve $D = \text{shift} \cdot q_0 (p/p_0)^{-\eta}$ on one product it is

    \[\int_p^{\bar p} D = \text{shift} \cdot q_0\, p_0^{\eta}\, \frac{\bar p^{\,1-\eta} - p^{\,1-\eta}}{1-\eta} \quad (\eta \ne 1), \qquad \text{shift} \cdot q_0\, p_0 \log\frac{\bar p}{p} \quad (\eta = 1),\]

    finite for every $\eta$. The ceiling is a convention, such as the price of a backstop substitute; the measure depends on it, so it has to be reported with the results.
