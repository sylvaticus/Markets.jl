Processes and substitution

Processes, nests and input substitution

A process is a transformation with constant returns to scale: per unit of activity it needs a fixed set of input requirements (its nests) and yields fixed quantities of one or more outputs.

  • Inputs in different nests are needed in fixed proportion to each other. If a process needs 1 unit of nest A and 0.2 units of nest B, it cannot use more of A to save on B.
  • Inputs in the same nest substitute for each other.

Leontief nests

A nest with a single product is a fixed coefficient (Leontief): exactly coeff units per unit of activity. Fixed proportions between several products are written as several Leontief nests.

CES nests

A nest with several products is a constant-elasticity-of-substitution bundle, the function of Arrow, Chenery, Minhas & Solow (1961). The aim is smooth substitution: if one input becomes more expensive, the process uses proportionally less of it, rather than switching entirely from one input to another as a linear model with alternative processes would do. For instance, a steel mill that uses 30% scrap at equal prices may use 20% scrap when scrap becomes more expensive, and the share moves continuously with relative prices.

The cost-minimising mix satisfies

\[\frac{q_i}{q_j} = \frac{\delta_i}{\delta_j}\left(\frac{p_j}{p_i}\right)^{\sigma}\]

where:

  • the shares $\delta_i$ (normalised to sum to 1) fix the mix at equal input prices: at equal prices, input $i$ makes up the fraction $\delta_i$ of the nest, both in quantity and in value. They are the calibration of where the substitution starts from. Without them all inputs would be used in equal amounts at equal prices.
  • the elasticity of substitution $\sigma$ fixes how fast the mix moves away from an input whose relative price rises. At 1 the mix is Cobb–Douglas (value shares stay constant), below 1 the inputs are poor substitutes, at 0 the mix does not move at all, and large values make the inputs nearly perfect substitutes.

The shares are written in calibrated form, so that the price of the CES composite equals the input price when all input prices are equal. As a result the equilibrium price of an output equals its true marginal cost of production.

Range of the elasticity of substitution

$\sigma$ may be any value $\ge 0$, here and in every other CES of the package — the Armington and OriginNest groups of origins and the DemandNest groups of final demand:

  • $\sigma > 1$: good substitutes. When an input's relative price rises, its quantity falls by more than its price rose, so its value share falls.
  • $\sigma = 1$: Cobb–Douglas. Each input keeps the value share $\delta_i$ whatever the prices.
  • $0 < \sigma < 1$: poor substitutes, or complements. The quantity falls by less than the price rose, so the value share of the dearer input rises.
  • $\sigma = 0$: fixed proportions (Leontief). Input $i$ is $\delta_i$ of the composite whatever the prices.

The aggregator is written with $\rho = (\sigma - 1)/\sigma$, which cannot be evaluated at $\sigma = 0$ ($\rho = -\infty$), at $\sigma = 1$ ($\rho = 0$) or at $\sigma = \infty$. At those three values the engine writes its limit instead, and the price of the composite is the limit of the CES index:

$\sigma$composite of the $q_i$price of the composite
$0$$\min_i q_i/\delta_i$$\sum_i \delta_i p_i$
$1$$\prod_i (q_i/\delta_i)^{\delta_i}$$\prod_i p_i^{\delta_i}$
$\infty$$\sum_i q_i$$\min_i p_i$

These are the exact limits of the calibrated CES, so an elasticity just beside 0 or 1 gives nearly the same equilibrium as 0 or 1 itself. Every one of them is concave, so the program stays convex over the whole range.

Two practical points. Below 1 the exponent $\rho$ is negative, so $q_i^{\rho}$ grows without bound as an input goes to zero; the quantity floor keeps the solver away from zero, as it already does above 1. And very close to 0 — below about $\sigma = 0.01$, depending on the data — $q_i^{\rho}$ leaves the range of floating-point numbers and the solve fails. Use $\sigma = 0$ there: at $\sigma = 0.01$ the equilibrium is already within a fraction of a percent of it.

A nest of a single product has nothing to substitute and is a fixed coefficient whatever its $\sigma$; leontief builds one and stores $\sigma = 0$. A fixed-proportion requirement of several products is ces(...; sigma = 0).

Value-added cost

vacost is the cost per unit of activity of everything not modelled as an explicit input: labour, energy, capital, chemicals. It is a constant, so these inputs have an exogenous price and cannot substitute for the modelled inputs. It may differ by region, as wages and energy do, and a vacost calibrated on base-year margins always will.

An input with an exogenous price that should substitute for the others — a chemical bought at a world price, say — is instead declared as a product with a flat supply curve, SupplyCurve(...; elasticity = Inf), in each region that uses it, and put in the nest beside them. Its price is then p0 whatever the quantity, and the quantity responds to the prices of the other inputs through the nest's $\sigma$.

Why processes, and not supply and demand per transformation

There is another way to write the same economy. Instead of a transformation with fixed coefficients whose scale the material balances determine, each industry could carry its own estimated output supply and input demand functions — ideally derived from a profit function, so that Hotelling's lemma makes them consistent — and prices would become unknowns of a square system rather than duals of a balance. That is the behavioural or equation-system paradigm, and the choice between the two is old enough to have a standard reference in McCarl & Spreen (1980).

Which one a model uses is largely a matter of which literature it grew up in. Forest-sector, land-use and bioeconomy models are mostly process-based, as the review in Latta et al. (2013) shows; agricultural outlook and trade-policy models are mostly behavioural, as the classification in van Tongeren et al. (2001) shows; and the large models in both camps end up hybrids, because a behavioural model that does not tie its input demands to its output supplies by a fixed yield can imply impossible physical conversions.

This package takes the process route, and the reasons are worth stating plainly because they are also its limitations.

What the choice buys. Every balance is an equality in physical units, and a recipe is a fixed yield: what goes into a plant comes out of it, as product or as residue, and no combination of estimated elasticities can violate that. The one qualification is aggregation: a CES bundle of inputs, or an Armington composite of origins, is an aggregate rather than a sum, and the physical account then needs one more line — see Physical accounts. The parameters are engineering coefficients, which exist for most transformations, where econometric estimates on intermediate markets usually do not. A technology with no history at all can be added by writing down its recipe. And because the problem stays a single convex optimisation, the equilibrium exists, the welfare measure means something, and one solve delivers both.

What it costs. Constant returns to scale mean a process has no supply response of its own. At the zero-profit price it would run at any scale, so its level is pinned entirely by the balances around it — never by its own profitability. A transformation in this model does not decide how much to produce; the rest of the economy decides for it.

What that looks like in practice

Sharper than "the cheapest region takes everything". Take two otherwise identical regions, each able to run the same process (ten units of input per unit of output, a value-added cost of 1300, freight of 5 on both products), and lower one region's value-added cost step by step:

cost in Aoutput in Aoutput in B
130079.879.8
124780.479.6
1245104.655.4
1243160.00.0

Two failures, not one. Over the first 53 units of cost advantage — 4% — nothing happens at all; then the whole industry moves over a change of 4 units, or 0.3%. Processing location is a step function: numb, then total.

The width of the numb zone is not arbitrary. A local processor is protected by the freight on everything it would otherwise import, which is the freight on the inputs multiplied by the recipe coefficient, plus the freight on the output: $10 \times 5 + 5 = 55$ here, and the step duly falls at $1300 - 55$. Raise the input freight to 60 and the protection becomes 605: a 46% cost advantage then buys nothing whatever. In a sector whose raw material is expensive to move and whose recipes take several units of input per unit of output — ore smelting, sugar refining, forestry — a model is normally deep inside the numb zone, so the practical symptom is inertia rather than volatility.

Why this is usually not the problem it appears to be

Because Armington restores the missing slope. If a product's regional varieties are imperfect substitutes, every region that makes it faces a downward-sloping demand for its own variety, and the step disappears. The same experiment, with $\sigma = 4$ on the output:

cost in Aoutput in Aoutput in B
125580.379.6
124380.479.6
120081.878.4
110085.775.2

Smooth, and gentle: a 15% cost advantage moves the split from 50/50 to 53/47. The forest example declares an Armington specification for every product, so between its blocs it does not exhibit the behaviour above at all. Read the caveat as applying to products you have deliberately left homogeneous, which in practice means intermediates for which no trade shares were available, and groups of regions pooled as perfect substitutes — where, once costs are calibrated, it takes a sharper form still.

Perfect substitutes and constant returns

Calibrating vacost on base-year margins puts every process that ran exactly at break-even, in every region. Between regions whose products are perfect substitutes — a homogeneous product, or an OriginNest with sigma = Inf — that turns the step above into a plateau: the location of processing inside the group is not decided at all.

The mechanism is simple. Take two regions of the group that both sell the input and the output into the same market — two regions of one country both exporting a raw material and the product made from it to the same neighbour, say. Move one unit of processing from the first to the second: the first exports the raw material it no longer processes, the second exports that much less, and the product changes hands the other way. Both swaps happen inside one market, at one delivered price, so the move costs exactly nothing, and so does the next one. Nothing in the model prefers one split to another; the solver stops wherever its path ends, and a cost difference of a hair moves the whole plateau. In a test on the forest economy of the examples with its French regions perfectly substitutable, a 0.1% change in the costs of the French mills emptied one region of sawmilling and nearly doubled it in another.

Whether a base year sits on such a plateau depends on its trade pattern. When each region of the group sells its output to a different market, moving processing between them shifts quantities between markets, whose demands slope, and the split is pinned; when they share a market for both input and output, as regions of one country selling to their neighbours commonly do, it is not.

There are three ways out, in increasing order of what they ask for:

  • A finite elasticity inside the group, however high. Each region's product then has a demand of its own, and the plateau tilts into a slope. The cost is that the group is no longer one market: products cross-haul between its regions, and producer prices may differ by more than the freight between them. The forest examples do this, with sigma = 30, and show both.
  • A value-added cost rising with activity, the principled remedy described in the next section. Each region's mill then has a supply curve of its own, so the split is decided by costs rather than left open, and the group can stay perfectly substitutable.
  • Capacities that bind at the base year. They pin the split, but only if set equal to observed output, which says every plant ran flat out — rarely true, and it forbids every plant from growing in any scenario. A capacity with room to spare leaves the plateau in place inside its bounds.

The fix that is not implemented, and why

The principled remedy, for the step and for the plateau alike, is not to abandon processes but to give them decreasing returns: replace the constant vacost with a cost rising in the activity level, calibrated so that the observed activity is what a processor would choose at observed margins. This is positive mathematical programming, the standard answer in agricultural sector models, and it would sit comfortably in this package — the objective term would take the same shape as the supply curves already do, the problem would stay convex, and an infinite elasticity would recover today's behaviour exactly. Between that limit and a Limit on the activity, which is the opposite limit of a perfectly inelastic process, the parameter would simply interpolate.

It is not implemented because of what it would need as data, not because of what it would need as code. Per process and per region it asks for a reference activity, a base-year margin — the difference between the output price and the input costs, which requires consistent price data on intermediate products in every region, the thinnest data in the sector — and an elasticity of processing supply, which is not an estimated quantity anywhere. Choosing it would mean replacing capacity bounds that were invented with an elasticity that was invented.

That trade may still be worth making, and for a model used to study where industry locates it probably is: a slope is a weaker assumption than a bound, it has an unambiguous direction, it can be swept in sensitivity analysis, and it makes the base year an interior optimum rather than a corner held in place by a constraint. But it should be made deliberately, and documented as a calibration choice rather than presented as measurement. Two details to get right if you do: the rising cost needs a positive intercept — an isoelastic cost alone makes the first unit free, so every region would produce a little of everything — and the inframarginal profit it creates has to enter the welfare measure, or welfare stops being comparable across model versions.

A full switch to the behavioural paradigm is a different and much larger decision. Independently estimated response functions are generally not integrable, so no objective exists and the equilibrium has to be posed as a complementarity problem and solved as one — Mathiesen (1985), Rutherford (1995), Ferris & Pang (1997), and [C4] and [C5] for the tooling. That route would handle ad valorem wedges exactly instead of by iteration, and would accept asymmetric cross-price effects; it would also give up the guarantee of existence, the single welfare number, and the mass-balance consistency that is the main reason this package is built the way it is.

Joint products and residues

A process can have several outputs. Residues (e.g. the slag of a blast furnace, or the fuel oil of a refinery) are ordinary products with their own material balance in each region, so they can be consumed, traded or used as inputs to other processes, and their price is determined by the competition between their uses.

The material balance is an equality: every unit of a by-product must be used, traded or consumed. There is no free disposal, and this has a consequence worth stating plainly. A by-product that nobody wants is a bad: somebody must be paid to take it, and its equilibrium price is negative. The model reports it that way, since the price is the signed dual of the balance.

That is the right answer to the question as posed, but it is rarely the question meant, and under a large supply shock it can take a local market somewhere surprising. If free disposal is what you want instead, give the by-product somewhere to go — disposal writes the process for you:

processes = [refinery, steel_mill,
             disposal(product = "fuel_oil"),          # free: price ≥ 0
             disposal(product = "slag", cost = 12)]   # paid: price ≥ −12

Its price then settles at zero rather than going negative, and a positive cost represents a real disposal charge — a landfill fee, an incinerator, a tailings dam. Deciding which by-products have a way out is a modelling choice the model cannot make for you, which is why there is no default. Writing the balances as inequalities, as a mixed-complementarity formulation would, is the other route to the same thing; it is not what this package does.

Substitution in final demand

Final demands are independent of each other by default: each product's curve responds to its own price alone. That is often too strong — butter and margarine go on the same bread, petrol and diesel cars do the same job on the road — and the question is how to let one product's price move another's demand.

The tempting answer, a matrix of cross-price elasticities, is not available, and for the same reason an arbitrary matrix of Armington elasticities is not. Demand here is the gradient of the consumer-benefit term of the objective; that is what makes the surplus-maximisation equivalence hold in the first place. A demand system that is not the gradient of anything — an arbitrary matrix is not, unless it satisfies the symmetry conditions — has no benefit function behind it, and the program it would be put into would no longer be the market it claims to represent.

What is available is the same answer as for origins: a tree, through DemandNest. Products that substitute closely sit in a group with a high $\sigma$; the group as a whole substitutes against the rest at the lower $\sigma$ of the level above; and the whole bundle faces one DemandCurve curve, which describes the composite. So

DemandCurve(region = "EU", p0 = 30000, q0 = 12, elasticity = 0.5,
           product = DemandNest(sigma = 1.6, products = ["electric_car"], shares = [0.25],
                       nests = [DemandNest(sigma = 5.0, share = 0.75,
                                           products = ["petrol_car", "diesel_car"],
                                           shares   = [0.65, 0.35])]))

says that petrol and diesel cars are close substitutes, that an electric car is a more distant substitute for either, and that cars as a whole have an own-price elasticity of 0.5.

The cross-price effects this implies are the CES ones: within a group, the compensated cross-price elasticity of product $i$ with respect to the price of $j$ is $\sigma s_j$, with $s_j$ the value share of $j$. A large supplier therefore transmits its price more strongly than a small one at the same $\sigma$ — the shares matter as much as the elasticity, exactly as they do between origins.

Substitutes outside the model

Cars compete with public transport, whose fares this model does not determine. That needs none of the machinery above: an exogenous price is a constant, so its effect on demand is a shift of the whole curve. Put the cross-price elasticity in exogenous_shift as the driver elasticity:

DemandCurve(product = cars, region = "EU", p0 = ..., q0 = ...,
           elasticity = 0.4,
           shift = exogenous_shift(fares = (level = 3.2, reference = 2.0,
                                            elasticity = 0.35)))

Fares 60% dearer than at calibration, with a cross-price elasticity of 0.35, raise demand for the car bundle by $1.6^{0.35} \approx 1.18$. The endogenous substitution inside the bundle and the exogenous substitution against public transport are different mechanisms, and it is worth keeping them visibly apart.
