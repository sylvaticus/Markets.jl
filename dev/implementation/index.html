Code implementation

This page describes the mathematical program built by solve_market, how results are extracted, and the layout of the code. The reasons behind the formulation are on the Modelling choices page.

Source layout

src/limits.jlLimit, LimitExpression, @limit and @limits: linear constraints on the quantities of the model

test/runtests.jltest suite
docs/this documentation (Documenter.jl, with Literate.jl for the example pages)

Every type of the data schema is declared with Base.@kwdef, so it is built with keyword arguments and its optional fields have defaults; the field documentation on the API reference page is generated from the struct definitions with DocStringExtensions. The two nest builders, leontief and ces, take keyword arguments too.

The model is written with JuMP ([C1], Lubin et al. 2023) and solved by default with Ipopt ([C2]), the interior-point method of Wächter & Biegler (2006). Variables and constraints are created only for the combinations present in the data, and stored in dictionaries keyed by the names themselves (e.g. D[(region, product)], T[(product, from, to)]), not in dense arrays.

The mathematical program

Sets and data

$\underline L_l, \overline L_l, w_{l,v}$the bounds of limit $l$ and the coefficient of variable $v$ in its expression
$y_{k,p}$yield of output $p$ per unit of activity of $k$
$\bar a_n, \delta_{n,i}, \sigma_n, \phi_n$composite requirement, shares, elasticity of substitution and scale of nest $n$
$\delta_{p,o,r}$value share of origin $o$ in destination $r$ (normalised over $O_{p,r}$)
$\mathcal{N}_{p,r}$the CES tree destination $r$ aggregates the origins of $p$ with: leaves are origins, each node $\nu$ carrying an elasticity $\sigma_\nu$ and shares over its children
$c_{k,r}$value-added cost per unit of activity of $k$ in $r$ (vacost, one number or one per region)
$\tau_{p,r,r'}$unit transport cost on a route

Variables

VariableDomainMeaning
$D_{r,p} \ge q_{min}$$(r,p) \in \mathcal{D}$final demand; bounded by 0 instead on a straight or a flat curve, whose marginal benefit at zero is finite

$X_{p,o,r} \ge 0$$p \in P^A$, $o \in O_{p,r}$quantity of the variety of origin $o$ used in $r$; floored only under a node with $0 < \sigma < \infty$
$A_{p,r} \ge 0$$p \in P^A$, $r \in R$composite of $p$ available to the users of $r$, built only where $r$ uses it
$C_{\nu} \ge 0$internal nodes $\nu$ of $\mathcal{N}_{p,r}$sub-composite of a group of origins; floored only when its parent has $0 < \sigma < \infty$

$q_{min}$ is, for each variable, $10^{-6}$ of that variable's own reference magnitude, or the one absolute floor given as the qfloor keyword of solve_market (see Numerical details).

Objective

\[\begin{aligned} \max\; & \sum_{(r,p) \in \mathcal{D}} \frac{a_{r,p}}{1 - 1/\eta_{r,p}}\, D_{r,p}^{\,1 - 1/\eta_{r,p}} \;-\; \sum_{(r,p) \in \mathcal{S}} \frac{b_{r,p}}{1 + 1/\varepsilon_{r,p}}\, S_{r,p}^{\,1 + 1/\varepsilon_{r,p}} \\ & \;-\; \sum_{k} \sum_{r \in R_k} c_{k,r}\, z_{r,k} \;-\; \sum_{(p,r,r') \in \mathcal{T}} \tau_{p,r,r'}\, T_{p,r,r'} \;-\; \sum_{p \in P^A} \sum_{r} \sum_{o \in O_{p,r},\, o \ne r} \tau_{p,o,r}\, X_{p,o,r} \end{aligned}\]

with $a = p_0\,(m q_0)^{1/\eta}$ and $b = p_0\,(m q_0)^{-1/\varepsilon}$: a shifter enters by scaling the reference quantity the curve is anchored at. The first two sums are the closed-form integrals of the inverse demand curve $P^D(D) = a D^{-1/\eta}$ and of the inverse supply curve $P^S(S) = b S^{1/\varepsilon}$.

CES nest constraints

For each process $k$, region $r \in R_k$ and nest $n$ with more than one product, with $\rho_n = (\sigma_n - 1)/\sigma_n$:

\[\phi_n \left( \sum_{i \in I_n} \delta_{n,i}^{\,1/\sigma_n}\, x_{r,k,n,i}^{\,\rho_n} \right)^{1/\rho_n} \;\ge\; \bar a_n\, z_{r,k}\]

The constraint is an inequality, which keeps the feasible set convex; at the optimum it is binding because inputs are costly.

Where the formula cannot be evaluated, the aggregator is replaced by its limit: at $\sigma_n = 1$ by the Cobb–Douglas $\prod_i (x_{r,k,n,i}/\delta_{n,i})^{\delta_{n,i}}$, at $\sigma_n = 0$ by $\min_i x_{r,k,n,i}/\delta_{n,i}$, which is written as the linear constraints $\phi_n x_{r,k,n,i} \ge \delta_{n,i}\, \bar a_n z_{r,k}$, one per input, and at $\sigma_n = \infty$ by $\sum_i x_{r,k,n,i}$. The same replacement is made in the Armington and the final-demand trees below; it is done in one place, ces_constraint!. A nest with a single product (Leontief) creates no variable and no constraint: the input use $\bar a_n z_{r,k}$ enters the material balance directly.

Material balance of a homogeneous product

For every region $r \in R$ and product $p \in P^H$:

\[\begin{aligned} & S_{r,p} + \sum_{k} y_{k,p}\, z_{r,k} + \sum_{r'} T_{p,r',r} \\ =\; & D_{r,p} + \sum_{k} \sum_{\substack{n \in N_k \\ I_n = \{p\}}} \bar a_n\, z_{r,k} + \sum_{k} \sum_{\substack{n \in N_k \\ p \in I_n,\ |I_n| > 1}} x_{r,k,n,p} + \sum_{r'} T_{p,r,r'} \end{aligned}\]

Terms are present only where defined (e.g. $S_{r,p}$ only if $(r,p) \in \mathcal{S}$). In the code the constraint is written as sources + imports − uses − exports == 0.

Balances of an Armington product

For $p \in P^A$ the single balance splits in two, because what a region produces and what its users consume are no longer the same good. Writing $\text{src}_{r,p}$ and $\text{use}_{r,p}$ for the source and use sides above (without the trade terms), for every $r \in R$:

\[\text{src}_{r,p} = \sum_{r' :\, r \in O_{p,r'}} X_{p,r,r'} \qquad\text{(origin balance: all local output is shipped somewhere)}\]

\[A_{p,r} = \text{use}_{r,p} \qquad\text{(absorption balance: the composite covers all local use)}\]

built only where the region uses the product at all — in final demand or as a process input. A region that uses none of it holds no composite and gets neither of these two constraints, though its origin balance remains so that it can produce the product for others. (Building them unconditionally made such a region infeasible, since $A$ carries a positive lower bound while $\text{use}$ is zero.)

\[\left( \sum_{c \,\in\, \mathrm{children}(\nu)} \delta_{\nu,c}^{\,1/\sigma_\nu}\, q_c^{\,\rho_\nu} \right)^{1/\rho_\nu} \;\ge\; q_\nu, \qquad \rho_\nu = \frac{\sigma_\nu - 1}{\sigma_\nu} \qquad\text{(CES at each node)}\]

one per internal node $\nu$ of the tree, where $q_c$ is $X_{p,o,r}$ for a leaf and $C_c$ for a group, and $q_\nu$ is $A_{p,r}$ at the root and $C_\nu$ below it. With no groups the tree is one node and this is a single CES over the origins. At the root the left-hand side is multiplied by the destination's scale $\phi_{p,r}$ (1 unless given), which divides the composite price by the same factor; armington_calibration sets it so that at the base year the composite equals the plain sum of the flows.

Note that $o = r$ is in $O_{p,r}$: a region's own variety goes through the composite like any other, and $X_{p,r,r}$ is its domestic use. A node left with one child contributes no variable and no constraint; in particular a destination with a single origin gets the linear constraint $X_{p,o,r} \ge A_{p,r}$, avoiding the powers.

The aggregator is the same calibrated CES as the input nests, so the price of each node is the standard CES index over its children,

\[\pi_\nu = \left( \sum_{c \,\in\, \mathrm{children}(\nu)} \delta_{\nu,c}\, \pi_c^{\,1-\sigma_\nu} \right)^{1/(1-\sigma_\nu)}\]

with $\pi_c = \pi^s_{p,o} + \tau_{p,o,r}$ at a leaf — the delivered price of that origin — and $\pi^c_{p,r} = \pi_{\text{root}}$ the price the users of $r$ pay. Flattened to a single node this is

\[\pi^c_{p,r} = \left( \sum_{o \in O_{p,r}} \delta_{p,o,r}\, \bigl(\pi^s_{p,o} + \tau_{p,o,r}\bigr)^{1-\sigma} \right)^{1/(1-\sigma)}\]

which tends to $\min_o (\pi^s_{p,o} + \tau_{p,o,r})$ as $\sigma \to \infty$, is $\prod_o (\pi^s_{p,o} + \tau_{p,o,r})^{\delta_{p,o,r}}$ at $\sigma = 1$ and $\sum_o \delta_{p,o,r} (\pi^s_{p,o} + \tau_{p,o,r})$ at $\sigma = 0$. At $\sigma = \infty$ the engine does not build these constraints at all: the product is treated as homogeneous, which is the same model.

How the tree is built

Groups of origins and groups of substitutes in final demand are the same structure, and the model builds both through one internal node type, CESNode — an elasticity, children that are leaves or further nodes, and the children's shares among themselves — and one builder, ces_tree!, which walks the tree, creates a composite variable and one ces_constraint! per node, and asks the caller for the variable of each leaf: an existing variety $X_{p,o,r}$ for an origin, a fresh consumption variable $D_{r,p}$ for a product. A node is floored only when the node above it raises it to a power, and a leaf likewise.

armington_tree turns the groups of a specification into that tree for one destination. The origins available to the destination are collected first, each with its share; every group is then reduced to the available origins it holds, and groups that keep none are dropped. Each node's shares are the summed shares of the origins beneath each child, normalised among siblings — so the data stay a plain bilateral matrix and the nesting only says how they are read. A DemandNest is read into the same form with its own shares. In both, a node left with one child is replaced by that child, at every level, so that the floors, the constraints and the printed model never carry a trivial group.

The specification applied to a (product, destination) pair is the one whose destination names it, or the one for every destination (:) otherwise, which is what lets the elasticity, the groups and the shares differ between importing markets.

Limits

Every ceiling, floor and fixed sum of the economy is a Limit: a linear expression in the model's variables between two bounds,

\[\underline L_l \;\le\; \sum_{v} w_{l,v}\, v \;\le\; \overline L_l,\]

where $v$ runs over the supplies $S$, final demands $D$, activities $z$, CES inputs $x$ and cross-border flows — the trade variables $T$ of a homogeneous product and the varieties $X_{p,o,r}$, $o \ne r$, of an Armington one. Production is $S$ plus yields times $z$, and what a fixed-coefficient nest draws is its coefficient times $z$, so both expand into these. A limit that is only an upper bound on one variable with a positive coefficient is set as a bound on that variable, $v \le \overline L_l / w_{l,v}$; any other is one constraint per finite bound, or an equality where the two coincide.

The rent of a limit is $\partial W / \partial L$, the change in the objective from moving the bound that binds: minus the dual of its constraint, or of the variable bound divided by $w_{l,v}$. It is reported only where the expression actually sits on the bound — complementary slackness, which an interior-point solver satisfies only to its tolerance. The rent per unit a variable carries is $\sum_l w_{l,v}\,\text{rent}_l$ over the limits it appears in, and its capacity the tightest bound a hard limit on it alone implies.

A soft limit, with a finite penalty $\pi_l$, gets a slack for each finite bound, $e_l^+, e_l^- \ge 0$:

\[\sum_{v} w_{l,v}\, v - e_l^+ \;\le\; \overline L_l, \qquad \sum_{v} w_{l,v}\, v + e_l^- \;\ge\; \underline L_l, \qquad W \;\mathrel{-}=\; \pi_l\,(e_l^+ + e_l^-)\]

It is never set as a variable bound. The slack is linear in the objective, so the program stays convex, and its first-order condition caps the dual at $\pi_l$: below the bound the limit behaves as a hard one, and once crossed its rent is the penalty. A bound counts as reached when the expression is on it or beyond it, and excess is $e_l^+ + e_l^-$. The penalty is reported apart, as $\sum_l \pi_l (e_l^+ + e_l^-)$, and added back to the objective in welfare.

Substitutes in final demand

A DemandCurve whose product is a DemandNest builds the same calibrated CES tree as the Armington one, over products instead of origins: one consumption variable per product of the group, one composite per node, and at each node

\[\left( \sum_{c} \delta_{c}^{\,1/\sigma_\nu}\, q_c^{\,\rho_\nu} \right)^{1/\rho_\nu} \;\ge\; q_\nu\]

The curve's benefit term is then taken on the root composite, not on the individual quantities, which is what gives the cross-price effects while keeping the objective a single concave function of the quantities — so demand stays integrable and the program stays convex. A group of one product collapses to the plain case with no composite and no constraint.

Tax wedges

A tax enters the objective exactly as a cost does, so each wedge modifies one coefficient that was already there. Writing $t_a$ for the summed absolute rates and $t_r$ for the summed relative ones on a given base:

OnEffect
supply$b \to (1 + t_r)\,b$, plus a term $t_a S$: the market must cover the marginal cost and the wedge
demand$a \to a/(1 + t_r)$, less a term $t_a C/(1+t_r)$: the curve is what the consumer pays, the gradient is what the market pays
process$c_{k,r} \to (1 + t_r)\,c_{k,r} + t_a$
trade$\tau \to \tau + t_a + \text{(ad valorem equivalent)}$

The first three are exact in one solve. The fourth is exact for $t_a$; the ad valorem part is a fraction of $\pi^s_{p,o}$, a dual, so solve_market solves repeatedly, each pass holding the tariff at the money equivalent implied by the previous pass's producer prices, until successive passes agree to within tax_tolerance (1e-5 by default). The model is built once: a pass changes only the tariff coefficients of the flows in the objective, and starts from the previous solution, primal and dual, with Ipopt told to stay there (warm_start_init_point, and a small initial barrier parameter) rather than move back into the interior. A pass after the first then takes a handful of iterations: on a 60-country version of the forest example (test/benchmarks/sixty_countries.jl), 5 to 18 against about 80 for a model built and solved from scratch, so that the whole loop with a tariff on every route costs little more than the untaxed solve. A warm start that fails is repeated from scratch. At the fixed point the wedge equals the rate times the origin price by construction, which is what a tariff means.

That loop is successive substitution, and it converges only where the map contracts. In practice it contracts hard — the price at the origin barely moves with the tariff, being set mostly by the exporter's own market — and rates of several hundred per cent settle in three to seven passes. There is no guarantee of it, though, so the step is halved whenever a pass increases the error rather than reducing it: a well-behaved problem never triggers that and runs at full speed, an oscillating one is damped until it settles. Beyond some rate a tariff becomes prohibitive, the route empties, and the price gap is then bounded by the wedge rather than equal to it — which is the equilibrium, not a failure of the loop.

Revenue is recomputed afterwards from the solution, instrument by instrument, and reported in Results.taxes. Results.welfare is the objective's terms evaluated at the solution with every tax left out. Adding the revenue back to the objective would give the same figure for per-unit rates only: a relative rate on supply or demand scales a whole curve integral, and the objective then differs from welfare by more, or less, than what the tax raises.

Convexity

The first term of the objective is concave for every $\eta > 0$: for $\eta > 1$ the exponent $1 - 1/\eta$ is in $(0,1)$, for $\eta < 1$ the coefficient and the exponent are both negative, and at $\eta = 1$ it is a logarithm; on a straight line it is a quadratic with a negative leading coefficient. The second is convex and subtracted — a power above 1, or a quadratic with a positive leading coefficient — and the others are linear, so the objective is concave. The CES aggregator is concave for $\rho < 1$, negative $\rho$ included, and so are its limits at $\sigma = 0$ (a minimum of linear functions) and $\sigma = 1$ (a weighted geometric mean with weights summing to 1), so the set where it is above a linear function is convex. The material balances are linear. The problem is therefore a convex program, and the local optimum found by Ipopt is the global one.

Validation

validate runs before anything is built, and solve_market calls it. It exists because of how the program is assembled: the balances are created by looping over regions × products, so a name outside those two lists gets variables and objective terms but no balance. A mistyped process input then becomes a free input, a mistyped consumed product becomes an unbounded one, and the solver reports success either way. Rather than guard each site, every name, duplicate and selector is checked once, up front, against the declared sets.

The same pass checks everything else that would otherwise surface only while the model is built, so that validate alone is the verdict on a specification: the Armington specifications resolve to one per product and destination (armington_of), with a product imperfectly substitutable everywhere or nowhere; every term of every limit selects at least one quantity the model will have (limit_terms over model_catalogue); every tax selects at least one curve, process or route; and the members of one demand group carry one ad valorem rate. It rejects two economies that have no finite answer to give: one with no regions or no products, and one containing a process with no inputs, no value-added cost and no ceiling on its activity, which is a free source of its own output.

From the solution to prices

The balance faced by the users of a region is stored in balance[(r, p)], and the price those users pay is the dual of that constraint:

dual(balance[(r, p)])             # the `price` column

The dual is the change in the objective per extra unit of product available to them, i.e. the competitive price. For an Armington product the origin balance is stored as well, in origin_balance[(r, p)], and its dual is what local producers are paid:

dual(origin_balance[(r, p)])      # the `producer_price` column
Do not take the absolute value, and do not use `shadow_price`

The sign is information, not noise. Because the balances are equalities, a by-product that nobody wants has a negative price — the amount somebody must be paid to take it — and an earlier version of this package reported abs(dual(...)), which silently turned such a price into its positive mirror image. shadow_price is no remedy: for an equality constraint it reports the gain from relaxing the constraint in whichever direction helps, so it returns the same positive number. The raw dual is the signed economic price and is what the code now uses.

The two prices differ by the composition of the CES bundle: local users pay the price index of all the varieties they buy, local producers are paid the value of their own. For a homogeneous product there is no origin balance and producer_price repeats price.

Either column is missing where the corresponding balance does not exist: a region that cannot produce an Armington product has no producer_price for it, and a region that uses none of one has no price, since it holds no composite of it.

Note

Because there is no free disposal, the price of a product in excess supply — a by-product with no profitable use — is genuinely negative, and is reported as such. Adding a zero-cost process that consumes it is how to ask for free disposal instead; see Joint products and residues.

Seeing the model

build_model (internal) builds the JuMP model and returns it with the variables and constraints the results are read from; solve_market solves what it returns, and jump_model hands it to the user unsolved. Every variable and every constraint is named — the balances balance[r,p] and origin_balance[r,p], the CES aggregators input_nest[r,k,n] for the $n$-th nest of a process, demand_group[r][i] and origin_group[p,r][i] for the $i$-th node of a tree, and armington[p,r], and limit[name] for a limit that is a constraint — so print(jump_model(d)) can be read against the formulation above. A balance in which no variable appears — a product the region neither makes, uses nor carries — is not written, since a constraint on nothing would only count against the degrees of freedom Ipopt insists on. A per-unit demand tax of zero is left out of the objective rather than written as a term that is zero, which would only make the printed model harder to read.

Results extraction

build_results (internal) reads the variable values after the solve and fills a Results with the tables:

  • production: $S_{r,p}$ plus $\sum_k y_{k,p} z_{r,k}$, for all region and product pairs with a value above tol, and supply for $S_{r,p}$ alone, with the capacity and the rent the limits put on it;
  • consumption: $D_{r,p}$ above tol, in composite and in physical units;
  • composites, inputs and recipes: the physical account of the CES aggregates, as described under Physical accounts;
  • trade: $T_{p,r,r'}$ and the cross-border $X_{p,o,r}$ ($o \ne r$) above tol;
  • net_trade: exports and imports summed over routes, for pairs with any trade above tol;
  • prices: one row for every region and product, if the solver returned duals, with price and producer_price as above;
  • activity: $z_{r,k}$ above tol;
  • limits and taxes: each limit with its value, excess and rent, and each tax with its revenue.

Every solved quantity, unrounded and unfiltered — the $x$ variables and the Leontief uses in drawn, the domestic varieties $X_{p,r,r}$ among the flows, production, and each region's composite — is kept in res.solution, which is what material_accounts and any account that must close exactly start from.

Numerical details

  • The quantity floor, qfloor. A variable that is raised to a power with an exponent below 1 has an infinite derivative at zero, which an interior-point solver cannot evaluate there. Those variables are therefore bounded below by a small positive qfloor rather than by 0, which forces a sliver of quantity that would otherwise be absent. It applies to exactly three kinds of variable:

    • final demand $D$, whose marginal benefit is $a D^{-1/\eta}$;
    • the inputs $x$ of a CES process nest, which enter as $x^{\rho}$, except in a nest with $\sigma = 0$, where they enter linearly;
    • the Armington varieties and sub-composites that sit directly under a node with $0 < \sigma < \infty$. A variety under a Leontief node ($\sigma = 0$) or pooled by an infinitely substitutable group enters its aggregator linearly and is bounded below by 0, so it can go cleanly to zero.

    Primary supply $S$, process activity $z$, trade flows $T$ and the composites $A$ need no floor and have none.

    Two consequences to keep in mind. A process with activity $z = 0$ still draws qfloor of each of its CES inputs, and its nest constraint is then slack rather than binding. And every origin–destination pair that is part of a CES node trades at least qfloor, whatever the prices.

  • The floor is set per variable. Each floored variable is bounded below by $10^{-6}$ of its own reference magnitude — the reference quantity of the curve it belongs to, the composite requirement of the nest it sits in, or the share of local absorption it is expected to carry. One absolute floor for the whole model would be chosen by the smallest product in it and then applied to the largest: add one obscure product with a reference quantity of 0.001 to an economy measured in hundreds, and every variable inherits a floor of $10^{-9}$, where gradients of the form $\rho x^{\rho-1}$ are large enough to give an interior-point solver trouble. Passing qfloor explicitly overrides the rule with a single absolute floor.

  • tol scales with the data. The threshold below which a quantity is not reported defaults to $10^{-3}$ of the smallest reference quantity in the economy, so that it means the same thing whether the model is written in tonnes or in million tonnes, and never hides a flow that matters for the smallest product.

  • Starting point. $D$ and $S$ start at $m\,q_0$, their shifted reference quantities; $z$ at 1; CES inputs at $\bar a_n$; and trade flows at 0. The solver moves a start that violates a bound back inside it.

  • The unit-elastic case. At $\eta = 1$ the exponent $1 - 1/\eta$ is zero and the power form of the benefit term is undefined; the code switches to $a \log D$ when $|1 - 1/\eta| < 10^{-8}$, which also avoids the cancellation that the power form would suffer just either side of 1.

  • Solver status. It is carried on Results.status, and by default anything but OPTIMAL, LOCALLY_SOLVED or one of the two ALMOST_ statuses is an error: an infeasible or unbounded economy returns tables that look exactly like those of a solved one, so returning them quietly is the dangerous choice. on_failure = "warn" or "ignore" restores the older behaviour. The ALMOST_ statuses, where the solver met its acceptable but not its target tolerance, warn and return.

  • Feasibility tolerance. Ipopt's constr_viol_tol is absolute, and its default of $10^{-4}$ can be coarser than the quantities of an economy written in small units — in which case a violated balance passes as satisfied and an infeasible model is reported as solved. It is therefore set to $10^{-2}$ of qfloor, and the acceptable tolerance to qfloor itself. Another solver needs the same treatment. Either can be overridden through solver_options.

  • Other solvers. Any JuMP solver that supports nonlinear constraints and duals can be passed with the solver keyword, and its options with solver_options.

Tests

test/runtests.jl checks the solution against conditions that hold at the equilibrium:

  • a one-product, two-region market: the exporting region is the cheap one, the price gap equals the transport cost, prices equal the inverse demand and supply curves, and regional balances hold;
  • a two-stage chain (a CES process feeding a Leontief process): the Leontief output price equals coefficient × input price + value-added cost, the CES input ratio satisfies the cost-minimisation condition, and the CES output price equals the composite unit cost + value-added cost;
  • origin groups: a group as substitutable as its parent changes nothing, the composite price matches the two-level CES index, a tightening group pulls its members' prices together, structures and elasticities differ by destination as specified, the : specification serves as the fallback, and inconsistent groupings are rejected;
  • demand elasticities on both sides of 1: the equilibrium price equals the inverse demand for $\eta$ from 0.3 to 3, the unit-elastic case matches the limit of the power form, a less elastic demand moves prices more, and non-positive elasticities and reference points are rejected;
  • prices keep their sign: an unwanted by-product that must be incinerated has a price equal to minus the cost of burning it, and adding a free disposal process brings it back to zero;
  • share calibration: armington_shares normalises per destination and reproduces the base-year sourcing through the CES demand condition, while differing from the observed shares themselves; ces_calibration inverts the CES demand condition, takes its limits at $\sigma = 0$, $1$ and $\infty$, and a demand nest built from it, flat or nested, has the base year itself as its equilibrium;
  • Armington trade: $\sigma = \infty$ reproduces the homogeneous solution exactly, the error against it falls monotonically as $\sigma$ grows, a finite $\sigma$ produces cross-hauling and producer-price gaps wider than the transport cost, the composite price equals the CES price index of the delivered prices, the variety mix follows the CES demand condition, a supply shock in one region moves prices and flows in the other, and the specification errors are caught;
  • elasticities of 1 and below: at $\sigma = 0$, $0.3$ and $1$ the process, Armington and final-demand nests satisfy the CES first-order condition and price index (or their limits), the $\sigma = 0$ and $\sigma = 1$ branches agree with an elasticity just beside them, a region whose own supply grows imports more of the other variety below 1 (complements) and less above it (substitutes), a Cobb–Douglas group inside a Leontief composite prices as the two-level index, and only variables raised to a power are floored;
  • nested Armington calibration: armington_calibration, on the flows and prices of a solved economy with a pooled group, rebuilds one that returns its prices, production and consumption, counts each composite in the units of the flows, and refuses a pool member that would have been the cheapest without shipping; ces_calibration's scale prices a bundle at the average unit price, and a vacost given by region gives zero profit at each region's own cost;
  • physical accounts: a region's composite is set against the varieties it received, every use of it is credited with their ratio so that the uses add up to what was received, homogeneous products need no correction, and each nest's physical inputs are set against its recipe;
  • limits: a cap, a minimum, an equality, a range and a limit with quantities on both sides hold at the solution; the rent of a binding cap equals the change in the objective when the cap moves, a minimum's rent is negative, the macro and the explicit constructor give the same equilibrium, a group's exports leave the group, and unknown names, quantities that do not exist, duplicate names and anything not linear are refused; a limit on one variable becomes a bound on it, the supply, activity and trade tables carry the rents of the limits their quantities are part of, Markets.scope names the same regions, products and kinds of variable before the solve as Results.limits after it, a family from @limits has one member per combination of its indices, named after them, and the colon selects every region or product while "all" is a name like any other;
  • material accounts: every region's material balance closes, and the world's — extraction equals consumption plus losses; the content of primary supply is the one given, region by region, what a region uses is the mix of what it received, a process loses its share and divides the rest among its outputs by their weights, a homogeneous product carries the content of where it was made, and inconsistent specifications are refused;
  • trade imputation: impute_flows meets production, use and known totals (single flows and group totals), keeps the gravity pattern up to one factor per origin and one per destination, scales uses to production, and refuses totals that contradict each other;
  • the forest example: written with uncalibrated numbers, it solves with all prices positive, world exports equal to world imports, and a storm that lowers prices most in France, then in the EU, then in the far blocs; calibrated on partial data, it lands within a per cent of every price and quantity and a few per cent of every customs flow; calibrated on complete data, it returns them to the precision of the solver.

using Pkg; Pkg.test("Markets")
