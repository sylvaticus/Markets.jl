Markets — Module
Markets

Solve a spatial partial-equilibrium model of an economic sector made of an arbitrary set of products, regions and transformation processes.

The package is the engine only: it knows nothing about specific products. You describe an economy with the data types MarketData, DemandCurve, SupplyCurve and Process (built from leontief and ces input nests), hand it to solve_market, and get back production, consumption, trade and prices per region in a Results object.

The engine implements a spatial price equilibrium (Samuelson 1952 / Takayama & Judge 1971) solved as a net-social-surplus maximisation. Under perfect competition this single optimisation is the market equilibrium:

  • the quantities (production, consumption, trade) are the primal solution;
  • the prices are the dual variables (shadow prices) of the per-region, per-product material-balance constraints.

Smooth (CES) input substitution, multi-stage transformation with joint products and residues, and trade between non-identical regional markets (transport costs) all fall out of the same formulation.

A complete example (the forest-products sector) is in examples/forest/.

Markets.Armington — Type
struct Armington

Imperfect substitution between the regional varieties of one product (Armington, 1969).

By default a product is homogeneous: it is the same good wherever it comes from, regional prices differ by at most the transport cost, and two regions never ship it to each other both ways (a region on the cheapest path between two others may carry it through). Listing a product here instead makes the varieties from the different origins imperfect substitutes: each region uses a CES composite of them, so a region can import and export the same product at once and its price responds to supply and demand everywhere, not only to the cost of the cheapest source.

Each destination r draws on the varieties of the origins it can buy from: its own, plus every origin o with a transport route (product, o, r). The composite is

A ≤ ( Σₒ δₒ^(1/σ) · Xₒ^ρ )^(1/ρ),     ρ = (σ-1)/σ

with Xₒ the quantity bought from origin o and δₒ its value share. The price a region pays for the composite is then the CES price index of the delivered prices,

Pᶜ = ( Σₒ δₒ · (Pₒ + τₒ)^(1-σ) )^(1/(1-σ))

A scale φ multiplies the aggregate, and divides this price, by the same factor.

which tends to the cheapest delivered price as σ → ∞: sigma = Inf is exactly the homogeneous (Samuelson spatial price equilibrium) case, and a large finite sigma approaches it. At the other end, sigma = 1 is the Cobb–Douglas composite Πₒ (Xₒ/δₒ)^δₒ, priced at Πₒ (Pₒ + τₒ)^δₒ, and sigma = 0 the Leontief one minₒ Xₒ/δₒ, priced at Σₒ δₒ (Pₒ + τₒ).

A single sigma makes every origin substitute equally well for every other, which is the defining restriction of a flat CES. Two ways out, both available here:

  • Groups of origins (OriginNest): origins whose products are mutually interchangeable are nested together with a higher sigma, and substitute with the rest at the lower sigma of this specification.
  • One specification per destination (destination): the structure may differ from one importing market to the next, which is what technical standards and non-tariff measures actually do, since the importer sets them.

Fields

  • product::String: Product whose regional varieties are imperfect substitutes

  • sigma::Float64: Elasticity of substitution $\sigma$ between the varieties of the different origins. It must be ≥ 0. Below 1 the varieties are poor substitutes, or complements; 0 makes each destination buy them in fixed proportions (Leontief) and 1 is Cobb–Douglas. Inf (the default for every product not listed) means perfect substitutes, i.e. a homogeneous product

  • shares::Dict{Tuple{String, String}, Float64}: Value shares $\delta$ of each origin in each destination's composite, keyed (origin, destination) — the shares that would be observed if the delivered prices of all origins were equal. They are normalised per destination. An origin with no share given (or a share of 0) is left out of that destination's composite. When this is empty, every origin available to a destination gets an equal share
  • destination::Union{Colon, String, Vector{String}}: Importing regions this specification applies to, or : (the default) for every region without one of their own. A product may have one specification per destination plus a : fallback, which is how the structure, the elasticity and the shares can differ between importing markets
  • nests::Vector{OriginNest}: Groups of origins that substitute for each other more closely than for the rest, as a vector of OriginNests. Every available origin not placed in a group stays a direct member of the composite. Empty (the default) gives the flat CES in which all origins substitute equally
  • scale::Union{Float64, Dict{String, Float64}}: Scale $\phi$ of the composite, one number for every destination or a destination => scale dictionary (a destination absent from it gets 1). With shares that sum to 1 the CES aggregate equals the plain sum of the varieties bought only where their delivered prices are equal, and falls short of it where they differ. A scale of the base-year sum over the base-year aggregate makes the composite count physical units again at the base year, at a price equal to the average delivered unit value, which is what lets base-year data in m³ or tonnes balance exactly; armington_calibration sets it. The default, 1, leaves the aggregate as it is

Example

# moderate substitution, equal shares, same in every market
Armington(product = "wine", sigma = 4)

# calibrated shares: 70% domestic, the rest split between the two other regions
Armington(product = "wine", sigma = 4,
          shares = merge(Dict((r, r) => 0.7 for r in regions),
                         Dict((o, r) => 0.15 for o in regions, r in regions if o != r)))

# in the EU market, EU and US cars are near-interchangeable (shared safety
# standards) while CN cars are a distant substitute; elsewhere everything
# substitutes freely
Armington(product = "cars", destination = "EU", sigma = 2.5,
          nests = [OriginNest(sigma = 12, origins = ["EU", "US"])])
Armington(product = "cars", destination = ["US", "CN"], sigma = 8)

Markets.DemandNest — Type
struct DemandNest

A group of products that substitute for each other in final demand.

Demand in this model is integrable by construction: it is the gradient of the consumer-benefit term of the objective, which is what makes the surplus-maximisation equivalence hold. An arbitrary matrix of cross-price elasticities is not integrable — it is the same obstruction that rules out an arbitrary matrix of Armington elasticities — so substitution between products is expressed the same way, as a tree. Products that substitute closely go in a nest with a high sigma; the nest as a whole then substitutes against the rest at the lower sigma of the level above, and the whole bundle faces one DemandCurve curve.

The implied cross-price effects are the usual CES ones: within a nest the compensated cross-price elasticity of product i with respect to the price of j is $\sigma s_j$, with s_j the value share of j, so a large supplier transmits its price more strongly than a small one at the same sigma.

Substitution against goods outside the model — public transport against cars, say — needs none of this. An exogenous price is a constant, so its effect is a shift of the whole curve: use shift with exogenous_shift and the cross-price elasticity as the driver elasticity.

Fields

  • sigma::Float64: Elasticity of substitution $\sigma$ between the members of the group. It must be ≥ 0, and at least the sigma of the nest enclosing it. Below 1 the members are poor substitutes, or complements; 0 makes them be consumed in fixed proportions (Leontief), 1 is Cobb–Douglas, and Inf makes them perfect substitutes — one good to the consumer, bought for whichever of them is cheapest

  • products::Vector{String}: Products belonging directly to the group

  • shares::Vector{Float64}: Value shares of those products at equal prices, in the same order. They are normalised, together with the share of each sub-group, over the members of this node. Equal shares when omitted

  • nests::Vector{DemandNest}: Sub-groups of the group, for a deeper tree

  • share::Float64: Weight of this group among the members of the group enclosing it

Example

## petrol and diesel cars are close substitutes; an electric car is a more
## distant one for both
DemandNest(sigma = 1.6, products = ["electric_car"], shares = [0.25],
           nests = [DemandNest(sigma = 5.0, share = 0.75,
                               products = ["petrol_car", "diesel_car"],
                               shares   = [0.65, 0.35])])

Markets.Limit — Type
struct Limit

A linear constraint on the quantities of the model:

lower ≤ expression ≤ upper

where expression is a LimitExpression — supply, consumption, production, activity, process inputs and trade flows, weighted by any numbers and summed. It can cap what several products draw from a shared resource, set a quota or a minimum, tie one quantity to a share of another, or fix a sum. With lower == upper it is an equality.

The limit is part of the economy: it is checked by validate, shown in its summary, and reported in Results.limits with its value and its rent — the gain in the objective from relaxing the bound that binds by one unit, positive for an upper bound, negative for a lower one: what a permit to one more unit would be worth, or the subsidy that would get agents to meet the minimum on their own.

Write it with @limit, or build the expression with the term builders, qualified as Markets.supply and so on outside the macro.

Fields

  • name::String: Name of the limit, used to report it

  • expression::LimitExpression: What is bounded: a LimitExpression, without its constant, which has been moved into the bounds

  • lower::Float64: Lower bound, -Inf (the default) for none

  • upper::Float64: Upper bound, Inf (the default) for none

  • penalty::Float64: Cost per unit of crossing a bound: Inf (the default) for a hard limit, which cannot be crossed, or a positive number for a soft one

Every ceiling of the model is one: the capacity of a plant, an allowable cut, what a port can ship. A limit that only caps one variable — one supply, one activity, one flow — is set as a bound on it, which costs the solver nothing; any other is a constraint of the model.

A limit is hard by default: no price can buy past it. With a finite penalty it is soft: it can be crossed, at that cost per unit beyond the bound, the way a sector model holds trade near its recent levels without forbidding a larger move. Below the bound nothing changes; past it the rent is exactly the penalty, which is also the most it can ever be. The penalty is a device of the model, not a cost anyone pays, so the results report it apart, in Results.penalties, and leave it out of welfare; a cost that is really paid past a threshold — overtime, an emergency import — belongs in the economy instead, as a second process or route at the higher cost.

Example

## an allowable cut, and the capacity of the mills, region by region
@limit "cut SEF" supply(region = "SEF", product = "logs") <= 3.5
[@limit("mill $r", activity(region = r, process = "sawmill") <= cap[r]) for r in keys(cap)]

## a port that ships at most 6 thousand containers, counted by product
@limit "port CN" sum(w * exports(region = "CN", product = p) for (p, w) in containers) <= 6.0

## two products harvested from the same stands cannot take more than they hold
Limit(name = "SEF stands",
      expression = Markets.supply(region = "SEF", product = ["swr", "smallwood"]),
      upper = 12.4)

## no more than a third of the sawnwood used in OF may come from abroad
@limit "OF imports" imports(region = "OF", product = "sawn_sw") <=
                    consumption(region = "OF", product = "sawn_sw") / 3

## imports held near last year's, unless it is worth 50 a unit to go further
@limit "pulp imports DE" imports(region = "DE", product = "pulp") <= 1.1 * last_year penalty = 50

Markets.LimitExpression — Type
struct LimitExpression

A linear expression in the quantities of the model: a weighted sum of QuantityRefs plus a constant. It is what a Limit bounds, and it is built by adding, subtracting and scaling the term builders — Markets.supply, Markets.consumption, Markets.production, Markets.activity, Markets.inputs, Markets.flow, Markets.exports and Markets.imports — by numbers. Nothing else is defined on it, so whatever can be built is linear: the product of two quantities, a quantity in a denominator, a power or any other function of one is refused.

Markets.MarketData — Type
struct MarketData

The complete description of an economy, to be passed to solve_market.

Fields

  • regions::Vector{String}: Regions of the model

  • products::Vector{String}: Products of the model

  • demand::Vector{DemandCurve}: Final demand curves, one DemandCurve per consumed product and region

  • supply::Vector{SupplyCurve}: Primary supply curves, one SupplyCurve per supplied product and region

  • processes::Vector{Process}: Transformation activities, a vector of Processes

  • armington::Vector{Armington}: Products whose regional varieties are imperfect substitutes, as a vector of Armington specifications. A product that is not listed here (the default) is homogeneous: the same good wherever it comes from

  • limits::Vector{Limit}: Linear constraints on any of the quantities of the model, as a vector of Limits: the capacity of a plant, an allowable cut, a ceiling on what a port can ship, a resource several products share, a quota, a minimum share, a fixed sum
  • taxes::Vector{Tax}: Taxes and subsidies, as a vector of Taxes, on production, on consumption, on transformation or on trade
  • transport_costs::Dict{Tuple{String, String, String}, Float64}: Unit transport costs, mapping (product, from_region, to_region) to the cost per unit of quantity shipped. This is also what declares trade: a route that is missing from this dictionary cannot be used, and a product with no route at all is not traded. Routes are directional, so a product that moves both ways needs an entry each way

Example

MarketData(regions   = ["EU", "US"],
           products  = ["crude", "gasoline"],
           demand    = demand,      # vector of DemandCurve
           supply    = supply,      # vector of SupplyCurve
           processes = processes,   # vector of Process
           transport_costs = Dict(("crude", "EU", "US") => 25.0,
                                  ("crude", "US", "EU") => 25.0))

Markets.Material — Type
struct Material

A material carried by the goods of an economy — carbon, dry matter, a metal, water — whose flows material_accounts traces through a solved economy, from the primary supply it enters with to the goods it ends up in.

The material enters with primary supply, at a given content per unit. It travels with the goods: a region's own production carries the content of what made it, and what a region uses carries the average content of what it received. In a process, part of what enters may be lost — burnt, emitted, discarded — and the rest leaves in the outputs, divided among them by allocation. Nothing else creates or destroys it, so what was extracted is what was consumed plus what was lost.

How much of the material a unit of a manufactured good carries is not given but follows from the solution: a mill that changes its mix of inputs changes the content of its output. This is the material-intensity accounting of Lorang & Gerlagh (2026), applied after the solve: the equilibrium does not depend on it.

Fields

  • name::String: Name of the material, used to report it

  • content::Dict{Union{String, Tuple{String, String}}, Float64}: Content per unit of primary supply, keyed by product — the same in every region — or by (region, product) where it differs, the more specific key taking precedence. A supplied product not listed carries none

  • losses::Dict{String, Float64}: Share of the material entering each process that leaves it other than in its outputs, keyed by process name; 0 for a process not listed. A process with no outputs, such as a disposal, loses all of it
  • allocation::Dict{String, Dict{String, Float64}}: How a process with several outputs divides the material leaving it, keyed by process name: a weight per unit of each output, as product => weight. By default every unit of every output gets the same content

Example

carbon = Material(name = "carbon",
                  content = Dict("logs" => 0.25),            # t C per m³ harvested
                  losses  = Dict("pulpmill" => 0.5),         # half burnt for energy
                  allocation = Dict("sawmill" => Dict("lumber" => 1.0, "chips" => 1.0)))

Markets.Nest — Type
struct Nest

One input requirement of a Process. Inputs in the same nest substitute for each other; different nests of the same process are needed in fixed proportion to the process activity.

  • A Leontief (fixed-coefficient) requirement is a nest with a single product: composite units of it are needed per unit of process activity. Build it with leontief.

  • A CES (smoothly substitutable) requirement bundles several products into a composite via a CES aggregator with value shares (δ, Σδ = 1), elasticity of substitution sigma (σ) and scale phi (φ):

    composite · z  ≤  φ · ( Σᵢ δᵢ^(1/σ) · qᵢ^ρ )^(1/ρ),     ρ = (σ-1)/σ

    As an input's price rises the optimiser smoothly shifts the mix toward cheaper inputs. Build it with ces. sigma may be any value ≥ 0: the formula is replaced by its limit at σ = 0 (fixed proportions, minᵢ qᵢ/δᵢ), at σ = 1 (Cobb–Douglas, Πᵢ (qᵢ/δᵢ)^δᵢ) and at σ = ∞ (perfect substitutes, Σᵢ qᵢ).

Use the leontief and ces builders rather than this constructor: they normalise the shares and check the arguments.

Fields

  • composite::Float64: Units of the (composite) input needed per unit of process activity

  • products::Vector{String}: Products of the nest. A single product makes the nest a Leontief one

  • shares::Vector{Float64}: Value shares $\delta$ of each product, summing to 1 (equal shares by default). They set the input mix when all input prices are equal. Ignored for a single-product (Leontief) nest

  • sigma::Float64: Elasticity of substitution $\sigma$ between the products of the nest. It must be ≥ 0: 0 for inputs needed in fixed proportions, 1 for Cobb–Douglas, Inf for perfect substitutes. It is ignored (and conventionally set to 0) for a single-product (Leontief) nest
  • phi::Float64: Scale parameter $\phi$ of the CES aggregator

Markets.OriginNest — Type
struct OriginNest

A group of origins that substitute for each other more closely than they do for the rest, inside an Armington composite.

Regions whose products are technically interchangeable — because they share grades, standards or certification, or because the same non-tariff measures let them through — belong in one group. Within it buyers substitute with the group's own sigma; against anything outside it they substitute with the sigma of the enclosing nest, which is lower.

Groups may be nested further through their own nests field, so the structure is a tree whose leaves are origins. This is the general shape an elasticity specification may take: the substitution between any two origins is the elasticity of the smallest group containing both, and a group must be at least as substitutable inside as it is with the outside.

Fields

  • sigma::Float64: Elasticity of substitution $\sigma$ between the members of the group. It must be ≥ 0, and at least the sigma of the nest that encloses it. Inf makes the members perfect substitutes: the group is pooled into a single good, as in the homogeneous case, while still being a distinct variety to the outside

  • origins::Vector{String}: Origins belonging to the group

  • nests::Vector{OriginNest}: Sub-groups of the group, for a deeper tree

Example

# in this market, EU and US products are near-interchangeable
OriginNest(sigma = 12, origins = ["EU", "US"])

Markets.Process — Type
struct Process

A transformation activity, operated at a level of activity chosen by the model.

Fields

  • name::String: Name of the process

  • inputs::Vector{Nest}: Input requirements, as a vector of Nests built with leontief or ces. Each nest is needed in fixed proportion to the process activity

  • outputs::Vector{Pair{String, Float64}}: Outputs, as product => yield pairs per unit of activity. Several pairs make a joint-production process (e.g. petrol and diesel from one refinery)

  • regions::Union{Colon, String, Vector{String}}: Regions where the process may operate, or : (the default) for every region

  • vacost::Union{Float64, Dict{String, Float64}}: Value-added cost per unit of activity: the cost of everything not modelled as an explicit input (labour, energy, capital, ...). A single number applies to every region the process operates in; a region => cost dictionary sets them one by one, and must then cover every such region. Wages, energy and capital do not cost the same everywhere, and a cost calibrated on base-year margins differs from region to region. The installed capacity of the plants, which bounds the activity, is a Limit on it

Example

## a refinery splitting each tonne of crude into three fuels
Process(name    = "refinery",
        inputs  = [leontief(product = "crude", coeff = 1.0)],
        outputs = ["gasoline" => 0.45, "diesel" => 0.35, "fuel_oil" => 0.15],
        vacost  = 40)

Markets.Results — Type
struct Results

The outcome of solve_market: the solved model and the equilibrium quantities and prices, as tidy DataFrames.

Use for_region to slice every table for one region at once.

Fields

  • data::MarketData: The economy that was solved

  • model::JuMP.Model: The underlying JuMP model, solved

  • status::MathOptInterface.TerminationStatusCode: What the solver made of it. Anything but OPTIMAL or LOCALLY_SOLVED means the tables below do not describe an equilibrium, whatever they look like; solve_market throws on those unless told otherwise

  • raw_status::String: The solver's own account of how it stopped, such as Ipopt's Solve_Succeeded

  • solve_time::Float64: Seconds spent solving, as the solver reports them, or as measured around the solve for a solver that does not. Summed over all solves

  • iterations::Union{Missing, Int64}: The iterations the solver took, summed over all solves: interior-point iterations for Ipopt, simplex ones for a simplex solver. missing for a solver that reports neither
  • solves::Int64: How many times the model was solved: 1, unless an ad valorem tax on trade had to be found by iteration
  • objective::Float64: The value of the maximised objective: net social surplus, with taxes counted as costs rather than transfers and the penalties of soft limits taken out, so not welfare as soon as either is present
  • welfare::Float64: Net social surplus at the solution, taxes counted as the transfers they are and no penalty: the objective's terms evaluated without any tax. The figure to compare across policy scenarios. With a demand of elasticity $\eta \le 1$ its level is defined only up to a constant, so only differences between runs on the same demand curves are meaningful
  • penalties::Float64: What crossing the soft Limits cost in the objective: each one's penalty times its excess. A device of the model rather than a cost anyone pays, so it is not part of welfare; zero without soft limits

  • production::DataFrames.DataFrame: Primary supply + manufactured output: region, product, quantity

  • supply::DataFrames.DataFrame: Primary supply alone: region, product, quantity, the capacity — the tightest Limit on this supply alone, Inf if there is none — and the rent, what the limits it is part of charge per unit of it: the scarcity rent of a binding capacity, or its share of a shared limit's

  • consumption::DataFrames.DataFrame: Final demand: region, product, quantity, in the units the demand curves and prices use, and physical, the same in the physical units of the flows. The two differ only for an Armington product, whose users buy a composite: see composites
  • composites::DataFrames.DataFrame: The composite of each Armington product in each region that uses it, against the physical quantity behind it: region, product, received (the sum of the varieties delivered there, its own included), composite (what its users draw on, final demand and processes together) and ratio = received / composite. A CES composite equals the sum of what it is made of only at the mix it was calibrated at; elsewhere ratio is the physical quantity a unit of composite stands for, and every use of the composite in the region is credited with it in physical columns. Value is exact on the composite side: composite times the price users pay is what was spent on the varieties, delivered
  • inputs::DataFrames.DataFrame: What each process draws of each input: region, process, product, composite (in the units the balances and prices use) and physical (the same in the physical units of the flows)
  • recipes::DataFrames.DataFrame: Each input nest of each process against its recipe: region, process, inputs (the products of the nest), recipe (the nest's requirement per unit of activity times the activity), physical (the physical quantity of its inputs, summed) and gap = physical − recipe. A recipe is met in composite units; the gap is the physical quantity the aggregation of imperfect substitutes uses up (positive) or makes up (negative) on the way, whether in the region's Armington composites or in a CES nest itself. It closes a physical account of the economy. The sum means something only where the products of a nest share a physical unit
  • solution::NamedTuple: Every solved quantity, unrounded and unfiltered, as dictionaries keyed as in the model: supply, production (supply plus process output) and consumption by (region, product), activity by (region, process), flows by (product, from, to) — a region's own deliveries of an Armington product included — drawn by (region, process, nest, product), and composite and ratio by (region, product): each region's composite of an Armington product, and the physical content of a unit of it. The tables above are built from these with small quantities left out; accounts that must close exactly, such as material_accounts, start from them instead
  • trade::DataFrames.DataFrame: Bilateral trade flows, positive ones only: product, from, to, quantity, and the capacity and rent the limits put on each, as for supply: a ceiling on a port or a corridor charges its congestion rent on every flow it covers

  • net_trade::DataFrames.DataFrame: Trade by region: region, product, exports, imports, net (= exports − imports)

Markets.armington_calibration — Method
armington_calibration(
;
    product,
    flows,
    prices,
    sigma,
    nests,
    rtol
)

The Armington specification of product that reproduces a base-year trade matrix exactly: its shares, through every level of the origin groups nests, and the scale of each destination's composite.

flows maps (origin, destination) pairs to the base-year quantity shipped, the diagonal included — what each region keeps of its own production, which trade statistics do not report and production minus exports gives. prices maps the same pairs to the delivered price: the producer price at the origin plus the cost of carrying the product to the destination, plus any duty charged on the way. sigma and nests are the structure the specification will have.

Each destination's tree is calibrated from the bottom up, by ces_calibration at every node: a group's price index and composite quantity make it one member of the group above, and an origin's share is the product of the shares on its path to the root. At the root, the scale is the base-year sum of the flows over the composite quantity, so that the composite counts the physical units the flows are measured in and its price is the average delivered unit value,

\[P^c_r = \frac{\sum_o X_{o,r}\, q_{o,r}}{\sum_o X_{o,r}},\]

which is the consumer price the base year implies. Calibrated on that price, the destination's demand curves and the input bundles of its processes buy exactly the base-year quantities.

A group of perfect substitutes (sigma = Inf) is a pool, bought only at its lowest delivered price: every member shipping to a destination must have that price there, to within rtol, and the members' shares do not matter, only their sum. An origin of a pool that ships nothing to a destination is kept in its composite if prices gives its delivered price there, so that it can start shipping when it becomes the cheapest, and the calibration checks that it is not the cheapest already. Any other origin that ships nothing is left out of that destination for good, as armington_shares does.

Every origin given a share must be able to deliver to its destination in the economy the specification goes into — produce the product, and have a route there — or the engine drops it and the shares no longer add up to what was calibrated.

prices = Dict(("FR", "EU") => 214.0, ("EU", "EU") => 205.0, ("US", "EU") => 238.0)

Markets.armington_shares — Method
armington_shares(; flows, prices, sigma)

Calibrate the Armington shares of one product from a base-year trade matrix, so that the model reproduces the observed sourcing at the observed prices.

flows maps (origin, destination) to the quantity the destination bought from that origin in the base year, its own output included as (r, r). prices maps the same pairs to the delivered price of that variety there, i.e. the producer price of the origin plus the cost of carrying it to the destination. sigma is the elasticity the shares are being calibrated for, any finite value ≥ 0.

The shares are not the observed quantity or value shares. Inverting the CES demand condition $X_o = A\,\delta_o (P^c/q_o)^\sigma$ gives

\[\delta_{o,r} \;\propto\; X_{o,r}\; q_{o,r}^{\,\sigma}\]

which is what this returns, normalised per destination: it is ces_calibration applied to the origins of each destination. Feeding observed shares in directly would only be right if all delivered prices were equal, since $\delta$ is by definition the share at equal delivered prices. From observed value shares s rather than quantities the equivalent form is $\delta \propto s\, q^{\sigma-1}$.

Pairs with a non-positive flow are left out, and an origin left out of a destination's shares is left out of its composite: a route unused in the base year stays unused, which is the well-known small-shares limitation of Armington calibration.

    prices = Dict(("EU", "EU") => 210.0, ("US", "EU") => 235.0, ("CN", "EU") => 250.0),
    sigma  = 4)
Armington(product = "wine", sigma = 4, shares = shares)

Markets.ces_calibration — Method
ces_calibration(; quantities, prices, sigma, rtol)

Calibrate a CES group from the base-year quantities and prices of its members, so that at those prices the model buys exactly those quantities. The group may be any CES of the package: substitutes in final demand (DemandNest), inputs of a process (ces) or the origins of a product (Armington, for which armington_shares does this for a whole trade matrix at once).

quantities and prices give the base-year quantity and price of each member, in the same order, and sigma is the elasticity of substitution the group is calibrated for. The result is a named tuple of three things the model needs and nobody observes:

  • shares: the value shares $\delta$ of the members, i.e. the shares they would have if all their prices were equal. Inverting the CES demand condition $q_i = A\,\delta_i (P/p_i)^\sigma$ gives $\delta_i \propto q_i\, p_i^{\sigma}$, normalised to sum to 1. They are not the observed shares: a member bought little because it is dear gets a large share, since it would be bought much more at an equal price.
  • price: the CES price index $P$ of the group, the price of one unit of its composite at the base-year prices.
  • quantity: the quantity $A$ of the composite, the base-year expenditure $\sum_i p_i q_i$ divided by price. It is an index number, not a physical quantity: m³ of firewood and tonnes of pellets do not add up, while their values at a common price do.
  • scale: $\sum_i q_i$ divided by quantity. Where the members are measured in one unit — m³ of wood of different kinds in a mill's input bundle — this is the scale $\phi$ that makes the composite count that unit again at the base year, at the average unit price $\sum_i p_i q_i / \sum_i q_i$: pass it as the phi of ces, with the base-year $\sum_i q_i$ per unit of activity as composite. Where the members do not add up it means nothing, and is ignored.

price and quantity are the reference point of whatever the group feeds: the p0 and q0 of the DemandCurve over a DemandNest, or, for a group nested inside another, the price and quantity of the group as one member of the level above.

Where the CES takes its limiting form, so does the calibration:

sigmasharespricequantity
0quantity shares $q_i / \sum q$$\sum_i \delta_i p_i$$\sum_i q_i$
1value shares $p_i q_i / \sum p q$$\prod_i p_i^{\delta_i}$expenditure / price
Infquantity shares, which do not enter the modelthe common price$\sum_i q_i$

Perfect substitutes are bought only at the lowest price, so with sigma = Inf every member with a positive quantity must have that price, to within the relative tolerance rtol. A member with a zero quantity gets a zero share, which DemandNest and ces do not accept: leave it out of the group.

Example

## (shares = [0.0505, 0.9495], price = 177.05, quantity = 4.151, scale = 2.023)

           product = DemandNest(sigma = 3, products = ["firewood", "pellets"],
                                shares = fuel.shares))

Markets.exogenous_shift — Method
exogenous_shift(; drivers...)

A log-linear shifter of a supply or demand curve, built from the exogenous drivers that move it and the elasticity of the curve with respect to each:

\[\text{shift} \;=\; \prod_j \left(\frac{Z_j}{Z_j^0}\right)^{\gamma_j}\]

Each driver is given as a NamedTuple with its current level, its reference level — the one the curve was calibrated at, which gives a shift of 1 — and the elasticity of the curve with respect to it. Pass them as keyword arguments, named for readability only.

This is the form that comes out of the usual estimation. Regressing $\log S$ on $\log P$ and on the $\log Z_j$ gives the price elasticity and the $\gamma_j$ in one go, so the shifters cost nothing extra to obtain once the curve itself has been estimated.

The shifter is the same on a straight line: it multiplies the quantity at every price, so $\gamma_j$ is the elasticity of the quantity to driver $j$ along the whole line, as along a constant-elasticity curve. A line estimated in levels, $Q = \alpha + \beta P + \gamma Z$, has its drivers in the intercept instead: there the driver moves alpha, by $\gamma (Z - Z^0)$, and is given as a new alpha rather than as a shifter.

Example

# a cod stock 18% larger than at calibration, and a slightly larger fleet

    stock = (level = 118.0, reference = 100.0, elasticity = 0.6),
    fleet = (level = 1.05,  reference = 1.0,   elasticity = 0.25))

Markets.impute_flows — Method
impute_flows(
;
    production,
    use,
    distance,
    decay,
    home_bias,
    totals,
    rtol,
    maxiter
)

A trade matrix for one product where the statistics do not give one: the flows that follow a gravity pattern as closely as possible while adding up to everything that is known.

production and use give, by region, how much was made and how much was used there — consumed plus taken in by its mills. Every region ships what it makes somewhere, itself included, and receives what it uses from somewhere, so these are the row and column totals of the matrix. They seldom agree exactly; the uses are scaled to the total production so that they do. The known totals below are met as given, so one that fixes the whole use of a region has to agree with that use once scaled.

The pattern is the gravity one: a flow between two regions falls off as $e^{-\text{decay} \cdot d}$ with their distance $d$, a function (origin, destination) -> d or a dictionary keyed (origin, destination) in either order, and a region's deliveries to itself are weighted by home_bias.

totals adds whatever else is known, as (origins, destinations) => quantity, each side one region or a vector of them: a single observed flow, ("NA", "EU") => 9.6, or a total over a group, such as national exports that customs report without saying which region of the country they left from, (["SEF", "SWF", "GEF", "OF"], "EU") => 12.5.

The flows are found by iterative proportional fitting — scaling them in turn to meet each total, until all are met to within rtol — which gives the matrix closest to the gravity pattern, in the entropy sense, that meets every total. A set of totals that contradict each other, such as exports larger than production, cannot all be met, and is an error after maxiter rounds.

The result, diagonal included, is what armington_calibration takes as flows, alone or merged with the flows that were observed.

Example

## from national exports to the rest of the EU
france = ["SEF", "SWF", "GEF", "OF"]

             use        = Dict("SEF" => 2.4, "SWF" => 5.1, "GEF" => 1.6, "OF" => 1.9,
                               "EU" => 8.2),
             distance   = (o, r) -> o == r ? 0.0 : 0.1,
             decay = 1.4, home_bias = 3.0,

Markets.solve_market — Method
solve_market(
    d::MarketData;
    max_tax_iterations,
    tax_tolerance,
    on_failure,
    kwargs...
) -> Results

Build and solve the equilibrium for the economy d and return a Results.

If the solver does not report an optimal (or locally optimal) solution, on_failure decides what happens: "error" (the default) throws, "warn" warns and "ignore" says nothing. The status, the solver's own message, the time spent and the iterations taken are on the Results.

  • solver_options are passed on to it, as a NamedTuple, a Dict or a vector of pairs: (max_iter = 500, tol = 1e-9), or Dict("max_iter" => 500, MOI.TimeLimitSec() => 60.0).   • tol is the quantity below which a flow, an output or an activity is treated as zero and left out of the result tables. It defaults to a thousandth of the smallest reference quantity in d, so that it scales with the units the economy is written in.

An ad valorem tax on trade is a fraction of the price at the origin, which is a dual of the problem and so cannot be written into its objective. When d has one, the equilibrium is found by repeated solves: each holds the tariff at the money equivalent implied by the previous solution's producer prices, and the loop stops when the two agree to tax_tolerance (relative), or warns after max_tax_iterations. Everything else is solved exactly, once.

That iteration is successive substitution, which converges only where the map contracts — quickly in every case tried here, including rates of several hundred per cent, but without a guarantee. The step is therefore halved whenever an iteration increases the error rather than reducing it, which rescues an oscillating case and leaves a well-behaved one at full speed.

Markets.Curve — Type
struct Curve{side}

A demand or a supply curve for one product in one region: side is :demand or :supply, and the two aliases DemandCurve and SupplyCurve are how a curve is written and read. The curve is given in one of two ways:

  • by a reference point (q0, p0) and the own-price elasticity there. The curve has constant elasticity by default, D = q0 · (p/p0)^(-η) or S = q0 · (p/p0)^ε, or is the straight line through the point with that slope, with form = :linear;
  • by the coefficients of a straight line, Q = alpha + beta · p, with beta < 0 for demand and beta > 0 for supply. A demand alpha is the quantity demanded at a zero price and must be positive; a supply alpha may have either sign: negative, nothing is supplied below the price -alpha/beta; positive, something is supplied even at a zero price.

Either way, shift multiplies the quantity at every price.

Fields

  • product::Union{String, DemandNest}: The product, or, for a demand curve, a DemandNest of products that substitute for each other, in which case the curve describes the composite of the group

  • region::String: Region where the product is consumed or supplied

  • form::Symbol: Shape of the curve: :isoelastic, the same elasticity at every price, or :linear, the same slope. Giving alpha and beta makes it :linear

  • p0::Float64: Reference price, i.e. the price at which the quantity is q0; NaN for a line given by its coefficients

  • q0::Float64: Reference quantity, i.e. the quantity at price p0; NaN for a line given by its coefficients

  • elasticity::Float64: Own-price elasticity at the reference point — $\eta$ of demand, $\varepsilon$ of supply — which must be positive. Any value is allowed: a demand with $\eta \le 1$ makes the benefit term of the objective an antiderivative of the inverse demand rather than the integral from zero, which leaves the equilibrium and the prices unchanged but makes the reported objective value a welfare difference rather than a level. Inf makes the curve flat, in either form: any quantity is bought or sold at p0, as an export market or imports at a given world price are (q0, which then plays no role, must still be positive, and shift has no effect). A flat supply that can reach a flat demand paying more than it costs, transport and taxes included, leaves the market without a finite answer. NaN for a line given by its coefficients

  • alpha::Float64: Quantity at a zero price, $\alpha$ in $Q = \alpha + \beta p$, for a linear curve; given, or derived from the reference point. NaN for a constant-elasticity curve
  • beta::Float64: Slope $\beta = dQ/dp$ of a linear curve, negative for demand and positive for supply; given, or derived from the reference point. NaN for a constant-elasticity curve
  • shift::Float64: Multiplicative shifter of the whole curve: the quantity at any given price is shift times what the curve otherwise implies. On a line, it scales alpha and beta together, so the price at which the quantity is zero does not move; a flat curve has no quantity to scale and refuses any shifter but 1. It is how everything other than the price enters — income, population and the price of a substitute outside the model for demand; the size of the resource, the roads and ports, the labour and machinery available for supply — leaving the reference point or the coefficients to hold the calibration. exogenous_shift builds one from drivers and their elasticities. The default, 1, is the calibrated curve itself. A hard ceiling on a quantity — an allowable cut, a quota, an exhausted resource — is a Limit instead

Markets.ces_constraint! — Method
ces_constraint!(
    m,
    qs,
    δ,
    σ,
    rhs,
    name;
    scale
) -> Union{Nothing, JuMP.ConstraintRef{M, C} where {M<:JuMP.GenericModel, C<:MathOptInterface.ConstraintIndex}}

Require the CES aggregate of the quantities qs, with value shares δ and elasticity of substitution σ, times scale, to be at least rhs. The aggregate is

( Σᵢ δᵢ^(1/σ) · qᵢ^ρ )^(1/ρ),     ρ = (σ-1)/σ

and, at the three values of σ where that formula cannot be evaluated, its limit there:

  • σ = 0, Leontief: minᵢ qᵢ/δᵢ, written as one linear constraint per member;
  • σ = 1, Cobb–Douglas: Πᵢ (qᵢ/δᵢ)^δᵢ;
  • σ = ∞, perfect substitutes: Σᵢ qᵢ.

Every one of them is concave in qs, so the set where it is above rhs is convex. The price of the composite is the CES index (Σᵢ δᵢ pᵢ^(1-σ))^(1/(1-σ)) of the members' prices, and its limits: Σᵢ δᵢ pᵢ at 0, Πᵢ pᵢ^δᵢ at 1.

The quantity of the composite that node stands for, as a variable of m: leaf(name, share, in_power) for a leaf — the variable of that origin or product, which the caller creates or looks up, told its share of the start value and whether it is raised to a power — and otherwise a fresh variable tied to its children by the CES aggregator of the node. start is the expected quantity of the node, divided among its children by their shares, and base names the constraints, numbered in the order the nodes are built.
