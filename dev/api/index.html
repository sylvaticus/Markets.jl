Markets — Module
Markets

Solve a spatial partial-equilibrium model of an economic sector made of an arbitrary set of products, regions and transformation processes.

The package is the engine only: it knows nothing about specific products. You describe an economy with the data types MarketData, DemandSpec, SupplySpec and Process (built from leontief and ces input nests), hand it to solve_market, and get back production, consumption, trade and prices per region in a Results object.

The engine implements a spatial price equilibrium (Samuelson 1952 / Takayama & Judge 1971) solved as a net-social-surplus maximisation. Under perfect competition this single optimisation is the market equilibrium:

  • the quantities (production, consumption, trade) are the primal solution;
  • the prices are the dual variables (shadow prices) of the per-region, per-product material-balance constraints.

Smooth (CES) input substitution, multi-stage transformation with joint products and residues, and trade between non-identical regional markets (transport costs) all fall out of the same formulation.

A complete example (the forest-products sector) is in examples/forest/.

Markets.Armington — Type
struct Armington

Imperfect substitution between the regional varieties of one product (Armington, 1969).

By default a product is homogeneous: it is the same good wherever it comes from, regional prices differ by at most the transport cost, and a region never both imports and exports it. Listing a product here instead makes the varieties from the different origins imperfect substitutes: each region uses a CES composite of them, so a region can import and export the same product at once and its price responds to supply and demand everywhere, not only to the cost of the cheapest source.

Each destination r draws on the varieties of the origins it can buy from: its own, plus every origin o with a transport route (product, o, r). The composite is

A ≤ ( Σₒ δₒ^(1/σ) · Xₒ^ρ )^(1/ρ),     ρ = (σ-1)/σ

with Xₒ the quantity bought from origin o and δₒ its value share. The price a region pays for the composite is then the CES price index of the delivered prices,

Pᶜ = ( Σₒ δₒ · (Pₒ + τₒ)^(1-σ) )^(1/(1-σ))

which tends to the cheapest delivered price as σ → ∞: sigma = Inf is exactly the homogeneous (Samuelson spatial price equilibrium) case, and a large finite sigma approaches it.

A single sigma makes every origin substitute equally well for every other, which is the defining restriction of a flat CES. Two ways out, both available here:

  • Groups of origins (OriginNest): origins whose products are mutually interchangeable are nested together with a higher sigma, and substitute with the rest at the lower sigma of this specification.
  • One specification per destination (destination): the structure may differ from one importing market to the next, which is what technical standards and non-tariff measures actually do, since the importer sets them.

Fields

  • product::String: Product whose regional varieties are imperfect substitutes

  • sigma::Float64: Elasticity of substitution $\sigma$ between the varieties of the different origins. It must be > 1; Inf (the default for every product not listed) means perfect substitutes, i.e. a homogeneous product

  • shares::Dict{Tuple{String, String}, Float64}: Value shares $\delta$ of each origin in each destination's composite, keyed (origin, destination) — the shares that would be observed if the delivered prices of all origins were equal. They are normalised per destination. An origin with no share given (or a share of 0) is left out of that destination's composite. When this is empty, every origin available to a destination gets an equal share
  • destination::Union{String, Vector{String}}: Importing regions this specification applies to, or "all" (the default) for every region without one of their own. A product may have one specification per destination plus an "all" fallback, which is how the structure, the elasticity and the shares can differ between importing markets
  • nests::Vector{OriginNest}: Groups of origins that substitute for each other more closely than for the rest, as a vector of OriginNests. Every available origin not placed in a group stays a direct member of the composite. Empty (the default) gives the flat CES in which all origins substitute equally

Example

# moderate substitution, equal shares, same in every market
Armington(product = "wine", sigma = 4)

# calibrated shares: 70% domestic, the rest split between the two other regions
Armington(product = "wine", sigma = 4,
          shares = merge(Dict((r, r) => 0.7 for r in regions),
                         Dict((o, r) => 0.15 for o in regions, r in regions if o != r)))

# in the EU market, EU and US cars are near-interchangeable (shared safety
# standards) while CN cars are a distant substitute; elsewhere everything
# substitutes freely
Armington(product = "cars", destination = "EU", sigma = 2.5,
          nests = [OriginNest(sigma = 12, origins = ["EU", "US"])])
Armington(product = "cars", destination = ["US", "CN"], sigma = 8)

Markets.DemandNest — Type
struct DemandNest

A group of products that substitute for each other in final demand.

Demand in this model is integrable by construction: it is the gradient of the consumer-benefit term of the objective, which is what makes the surplus-maximisation equivalence hold. An arbitrary matrix of cross-price elasticities is not integrable — it is the same obstruction that rules out an arbitrary matrix of Armington elasticities — so substitution between products is expressed the same way, as a tree. Products that substitute closely go in a nest with a high sigma; the nest as a whole then substitutes against the rest at the lower sigma of the level above, and the whole bundle faces one DemandSpec curve.

The implied cross-price effects are the usual CES ones: within a nest the compensated cross-price elasticity of product i with respect to the price of j is $\sigma s_j$, with s_j the value share of j, so a large supplier transmits its price more strongly than a small one at the same sigma.

Substitution against goods outside the model — public transport against cars, say — needs none of this. An exogenous price is a constant, so its effect is a shift of the whole curve: use shift with exogenous_shift and the cross-price elasticity as the driver elasticity.

Fields

  • sigma::Float64: Elasticity of substitution $\sigma$ between the members of the group. It must be > 1, and at least the sigma of the nest enclosing it. Inf makes the members perfect substitutes — one good to the consumer, bought for whichever of them is cheapest

  • products::Vector{String}: Products belonging directly to the group

  • shares::Vector{Float64}: Value shares of those products at equal prices, in the same order. They are normalised, together with the share of each sub-group, over the members of this node. Equal shares when omitted

  • nests::Vector{DemandNest}: Sub-groups of the group, for a deeper tree

  • share::Float64: Weight of this group among the members of the group enclosing it

Example

## petrol and diesel cars are close substitutes; an electric car is a more
## distant one for both
DemandNest(sigma = 1.6, products = ["electric_car"], shares = [0.25],
           nests = [DemandNest(sigma = 5.0, share = 0.75,
                               products = ["petrol_car", "diesel_car"],
                               shares   = [0.65, 0.35])])

Markets.DemandSpec — Type
struct DemandSpec

Constant-elasticity final demand for one product in one region, calibrated to pass through the reference point (q0, p0).

Inverse demand is P = a · D^(-1/η) with a = p0 · q0^(1/η).

Fields

  • product::Union{String, DemandNest}: The consumed product, or a DemandNest of products that substitute for each other, in which case the reference point and the elasticity below describe the composite of the group

  • region::String: Region where the product is consumed

  • p0::Float64: Reference price, i.e. the price at which the quantity demanded is q0

  • q0::Float64: Reference quantity, i.e. the quantity demanded at price p0

  • elasticity::Float64: Own-price demand elasticity $\eta$, which must be positive. Any value is allowed: for $\eta \le 1$ the benefit term of the objective is an antiderivative of the inverse demand rather than the integral from zero, which leaves the equilibrium and the prices unchanged but makes the reported objective value a welfare difference rather than a level

  • shift::Float64: Multiplicative shifter of the whole curve: the quantity demanded at any given price is shift times what the reference point implies. It is how everything other than the price enters demand — income, population, the price of a substitute outside the model — leaving q0 to hold the calibration. exogenous_shift builds one from drivers and their elasticities. The default, 1, is the calibrated curve itself

Example

DemandSpec(product = "gasoline", region = "EU", p0 = 900, q0 = 80, elasticity = 0.3)

Markets.MarketData — Type
struct MarketData

The complete description of an economy, to be passed to solve_market.

Fields

  • regions::Vector{String}: Regions of the model

  • products::Vector{String}: Products of the model

  • demand::Vector{DemandSpec}: Final demand curves, one DemandSpec per consumed product and region

  • supply::Vector{SupplySpec}: Primary supply curves, one SupplySpec per supplied product and region

  • processes::Vector{Process}: Transformation activities, a vector of Processes

  • armington::Vector{Armington}: Products whose regional varieties are imperfect substitutes, as a vector of Armington specifications. A product that is not listed here (the default) is homogeneous: the same good wherever it comes from

  • trade_limits::Vector{TradeLimit}: Ceilings on what can be carried between regions, as a vector of TradeLimits. Each caps the weighted sum of the flows it covers
  • taxes::Vector{Tax}: Taxes and subsidies, as a vector of Taxes, on production, on consumption, on transformation or on trade
  • transport_costs::Dict{Tuple{String, String, String}, Float64}: Unit transport costs, mapping (product, from_region, to_region) to the cost per unit of quantity shipped. This is also what declares trade: a route that is missing from this dictionary cannot be used, and a product with no route at all is not traded. Routes are directional, so a product that moves both ways needs an entry each way

Example

MarketData(regions   = ["EU", "US"],
           products  = ["crude", "gasoline"],
           demand    = demand,      # vector of DemandSpec
           supply    = supply,      # vector of SupplySpec
           processes = processes,   # vector of Process
           transport_costs = Dict(("crude", "EU", "US") => 25.0,
                                  ("crude", "US", "EU") => 25.0))

Markets.Nest — Type
struct Nest

One input requirement of a Process. Inputs in the same nest substitute for each other; different nests of the same process are needed in fixed proportion to the process activity.

  • A Leontief (fixed-coefficient) requirement is a nest with a single product: composite units of it are needed per unit of process activity. Build it with leontief.

  • A CES (smoothly substitutable) requirement bundles several products into a composite via a CES aggregator with value shares (δ, Σδ = 1), elasticity of substitution sigma (σ) and scale phi (φ):

    composite · z  ≤  φ · ( Σᵢ δᵢ^(1/σ) · qᵢ^ρ )^(1/ρ),     ρ = (σ-1)/σ

    As an input's price rises the optimiser smoothly shifts the mix toward cheaper inputs. Build it with ces, which requires sigma > 1.

Use the leontief and ces builders rather than this constructor: they normalise the shares and check the arguments.

Fields

  • composite::Float64: Units of the (composite) input needed per unit of process activity

  • products::Vector{String}: Products of the nest. A single product makes the nest a Leontief one

  • shares::Vector{Float64}: Value shares $\delta$ of each product, summing to 1 (equal shares by default). They set the input mix when all input prices are equal. Ignored for a single-product (Leontief) nest

  • sigma::Float64: Elasticity of substitution $\sigma$ between the products of the nest. It must be > 1, or Inf for inputs that are perfect substitutes; it is ignored (and conventionally set to 0) for a single-product (Leontief) nest
  • phi::Float64: Scale parameter $\phi$ of the CES aggregator

Markets.OriginNest — Type
struct OriginNest

A group of origins that substitute for each other more closely than they do for the rest, inside an Armington composite.

Regions whose products are technically interchangeable — because they share grades, standards or certification, or because the same non-tariff measures let them through — belong in one group. Within it buyers substitute with the group's own sigma; against anything outside it they substitute with the sigma of the enclosing nest, which is lower.

Groups may be nested further through their own nests field, so the structure is a tree whose leaves are origins. This is the general shape an elasticity specification may take: the substitution between any two origins is the elasticity of the smallest group containing both, and a group must be at least as substitutable inside as it is with the outside.

Fields

  • sigma::Float64: Elasticity of substitution $\sigma$ between the members of the group. It must be > 1, and at least the sigma of the nest that encloses it. Inf makes the members perfect substitutes: the group is pooled into a single good, as in the homogeneous case, while still being a distinct variety to the outside

  • origins::Vector{String}: Origins belonging to the group

  • nests::Vector{OriginNest}: Sub-groups of the group, for a deeper tree

Example

# in this market, EU and US products are near-interchangeable
OriginNest(sigma = 12, origins = ["EU", "US"])

Markets.Process — Type
struct Process

A transformation activity, operated at a level of activity chosen by the model.

Fields

  • name::String: Name of the process

  • inputs::Vector{Nest}: Input requirements, as a vector of Nests built with leontief or ces. Each nest is needed in fixed proportion to the process activity

  • outputs::Vector{Pair{String, Float64}}: Outputs, as product => yield pairs per unit of activity. Several pairs make a joint-production process (e.g. petrol and diesel from one refinery)

  • regions::Union{String, Vector{String}}: Regions where the process may operate, or "all" for every region

  • vacost::Float64: Value-added cost per unit of activity: the cost of everything not modelled as an explicit input (labour, energy, capital, ...)

  • capacity::Union{Float64, Dict{String, Float64}}: Upper bound on the activity level — the installed capacity of the plants that exist. A single number applies to every region the process operates in; a region => bound dictionary sets them one by one, and a region absent from it is unbounded. Inf (the default) leaves the process unconstrained, which lets it concentrate wherever production is cheapest

Example

## a refinery splitting each tonne of crude into three fuels
Process(name    = "refinery",
        inputs  = [leontief(product = "crude", coeff = 1.0)],
        outputs = ["gasoline" => 0.45, "diesel" => 0.35, "fuel_oil" => 0.15],
        vacost  = 40)

Markets.Results — Type
struct Results

The outcome of solve_market: the solved model and the equilibrium quantities and prices, as tidy DataFrames.

Use for_region to slice every table for one region at once.

Fields

  • data::MarketData: The economy that was solved

  • model::JuMP.Model: The underlying JuMP model, solved

  • status::MathOptInterface.TerminationStatusCode: What the solver made of it. Anything but OPTIMAL or LOCALLY_SOLVED means the tables below do not describe an equilibrium, whatever they look like; solve_market throws on those unless told otherwise

  • raw_status::String: The solver's own account of how it stopped, such as Ipopt's Solve_Succeeded

  • solve_time::Float64: Seconds spent solving, as the solver reports them, or as measured around the solve for a solver that does not. Summed over all solves

  • iterations::Union{Missing, Int64}: The iterations the solver took, summed over all solves: interior-point iterations for Ipopt, simplex ones for a simplex solver. missing for a solver that reports neither
  • solves::Int64: How many times the model was solved: 1, unless an ad valorem tax on trade had to be found by iteration
  • objective::Float64: The value of the maximised objective: net social surplus, less any tax revenue, which the program counts as a cost rather than a transfer
  • welfare::Float64: The objective with the tax revenue added back, which is the surplus the economy actually enjoys and the figure to compare across policy scenarios

  • production::DataFrames.DataFrame: Primary supply + manufactured output: region, product, quantity

  • supply::DataFrames.DataFrame: Primary supply alone: region, product, quantity, the capacity bounding it and the scarcity rent a binding capacity earns per unit

  • consumption::DataFrames.DataFrame: Final demand: region, product, quantity

  • trade::DataFrames.DataFrame: Bilateral trade flows, positive ones only: product, from, to, quantity

  • net_trade::DataFrames.DataFrame: Trade by region: region, product, exports, imports, net (= exports − imports)

  • prices::DataFrames.DataFrame: Equilibrium prices, the duals of the balances: region, product, price (what local users pay) and producer_price (what local producers get). The two differ only for an Armington product, where users buy a composite of the regional varieties

  • activity::DataFrames.DataFrame: Process activity levels: region, process, level, the capacity bounding it and the quasi-rent a binding capacity earns per unit of activity
  • limits::DataFrames.DataFrame: Ceilings on trade: limit, its capacity, how much of it is used, and the congestion rent an extra unit of it would be worth. Empty when the economy has no TradeLimit
  • taxes::DataFrames.DataFrame: What each Tax raises: tax, what it falls on, and its revenue (negative for a subsidy). Adding the total back to the objective recovers welfare, which a taxed objective otherwise understates by exactly this much

Markets.SupplySpec — Type
struct SupplySpec

Constant-elasticity primary supply (e.g. crude oil from wells, ore from a mine, fish from the sea, crops from land) for one product in one region, calibrated to pass through the reference point (q0, p0).

Inverse supply is P = b · S^(1/ε) with b = p0 · q0^(-1/ε).

Fields

  • product::String: Supplied product

  • region::String: Region where the product is supplied

  • p0::Float64: Reference price, i.e. the price at which the quantity supplied is q0

  • q0::Float64: Reference quantity, i.e. the quantity supplied at price p0

  • elasticity::Float64: Own-price supply elasticity $\varepsilon$, which must be positive

  • shift::Float64: Multiplicative shifter of the whole curve: the quantity supplied at any given price is shift times what the reference point implies. It is how the exogenous conditions of production enter — the size of the resource, the roads and ports, the labour and machinery available — leaving q0 to hold the calibration. exogenous_shift builds one from drivers and their elasticities. The default, 1, is the calibrated curve itself

  • capacity::Float64: Hard ceiling on the quantity supplied, whatever the price: an allowable cut, a licensed quota, an exhausted resource. Where a shifter scales the curve, this truncates it, and the gap that opens between the price and the marginal cost on the curve is the scarcity rent. Inf (the default) leaves the curve unbounded

Example

SupplySpec(product = "crude", region = "US", p0 = 550, q0 = 600, elasticity = 0.4)

Markets.Tax — Type
struct Tax

A tax or a subsidy — a subsidy being a negative rate — driving a wedge between what one side of a transaction pays and what the other receives.

on says what the wedge falls on:

onthe wedge sits betweenselected by
"supply"the market price and what primary producers receiveproduct, region
"demand"what consumers pay and the market priceproduct, region
"process"a transformation and its value-added costprocess, region
"trade"the price at the origin and the cost of landing a goodproduct, from, to

rate is per unit of the base when relative is false — per tonne produced, per unit of activity, per tonne shipped — and a fraction of it when relative is true: of the price received for "supply", of the price paid for "demand", of the value-added cost for "process", of the price at the origin for "trade". Selectors left at "all" match everything, and several taxes may fall on the same base, in which case the absolute rates add and so do the relative ones.

A taxed equilibrium is not a welfare maximum

The quantities and prices a taxed model returns are the right ones: the wedges enter the program exactly as a cost would, and its first-order conditions are the distorted market's. The objective value is not welfare any more, because it treats tax revenue as a cost consumed rather than as a transfer received. To get back to welfare, add the revenue reported in Results.taxes to the objective.

Ad valorem rates on trade are solved by iteration

An ad valorem tariff is a fraction of the price at the origin, which is a dual of the problem and so cannot be written into its objective. For those, and only those, solve_market solves repeatedly, each time holding the tariff at the money equivalent implied by the previous solution's prices, until the two agree. Every other combination is exact in one solve.

Fields

  • name::String: Name of the tax, used to report its revenue

  • on::String: What the wedge falls on: "supply", "demand", "process" or "trade"

  • rate::Float64: Size of the wedge, negative for a subsidy

  • relative::Bool: Whether rate is a fraction of the base rather than an amount per unit

  • product::Union{String, Vector{String}}: Products it falls on, or "all"

  • region::Union{String, Vector{String}}: Regions it falls on, or "all" — the producing or consuming region

  • process::Union{String, Vector{String}}: Processes it falls on, or "all"

  • from::Union{String, Vector{String}}: Origins, for a tax on trade

  • to::Union{String, Vector{String}}: Destinations, for a tax on trade

Example

Tax(name = "oil_royalty",   on = "supply",  product = "crude", region = "US", rate = 4.0)
Tax(name = "fuel_vat",      on = "demand",  product = "gasoline", rate = 0.2, relative = true)
Tax(name = "green_steel",   on = "process", process = "steel_mill", region = "EU", rate = -15.0)
Tax(name = "steel_tariff",  on = "trade",   product = "steel", to = "US", rate = 0.25,
    relative = true)

Markets.TradeLimit — Type
struct TradeLimit

A ceiling on how much can be carried between regions: the capacity of a port, a corridor or a border crossing, or a quota.

The limit applies to the sum of the flows it covers, so one entry can cap a single product on a single route, or everything crossing a border, or a set of products over a set of routes. Domestic use never counts against it: only flows that actually move between regions do.

Fields

  • name::String: Name of the limit, used to report it

  • products::Union{String, Vector{String}}: Products it covers, or "all"

  • from::Union{String, Vector{String}}: Origins it covers, or "all"

  • to::Union{String, Vector{String}}: Destinations it covers, or "all"

  • capacity::Float64: The ceiling itself, in the units the weighted sum is measured in

  • weights::Dict{String, Float64}: Per-product weights converting quantities into the unit the ceiling is expressed in — containers per tonne for a port limit in containers, say. Products absent from it count with a weight of 1, which is what a limit covering a single product, or products already in one unit, needs

Example

## one product, one route
TradeLimit(name = "steel_quota", products = "steel", from = "CN", to = "EU",
           capacity = 12.0)

## everything shipped out of CN, counted in containers through the weights
TradeLimit(name = "container_port", from = "CN", capacity = 6.0,
           weights = Dict("cars" => 2.0, "steel" => 0.05, "wine" => 0.04))

Markets.armington_shares — Method
armington_shares(; flows, prices, sigma)

Calibrate the Armington shares of one product from a base-year trade matrix, so that the model reproduces the observed sourcing at the observed prices.

flows maps (origin, destination) to the quantity the destination bought from that origin in the base year, its own output included as (r, r). prices maps the same pairs to the delivered price of that variety there, i.e. the producer price of the origin plus the cost of carrying it to the destination. sigma is the elasticity the shares are being calibrated for.

The shares are not the observed quantity or value shares. Inverting the CES demand condition $X_o = A\,\delta_o (P^c/q_o)^\sigma$ gives

\[\delta_{o,r} \;\propto\; X_{o,r}\; q_{o,r}^{\,\sigma}\]

which is what this returns, normalised per destination. Feeding observed shares in directly would only be right if all delivered prices were equal, since $\delta$ is by definition the share at equal delivered prices. From observed value shares s rather than quantities the equivalent form is $\delta \propto s\, q^{\sigma-1}$.

Pairs with a non-positive flow are left out, and an origin left out of a destination's shares is left out of its composite: a route unused in the base year stays unused, which is the well-known small-shares limitation of Armington calibration.

Example

shares = armington_shares(
    flows  = Dict(("EU", "EU") => 80.0, ("US", "EU") => 15.0, ("CN", "EU") => 5.0),
    prices = Dict(("EU", "EU") => 210.0, ("US", "EU") => 235.0, ("CN", "EU") => 250.0),
    sigma  = 4)
Armington(product = "wine", sigma = 4, shares = shares)
source
Markets.ces — Method
ces(; composite, products, shares, sigma, phi)

Build a smoothly substitutable (CES) input requirement of composite units of a bundle of products per unit of process activity.

shares are the value shares of each input when all input prices are equal (equal shares by default); they need not sum to 1, as they are normalised internally. sigma is the elasticity of substitution and must be > 1.

Example

## 1.1 t of a mix of scrap and pig iron per tonne of steel

    shares = [0.4, 0.6], sigma = 2.0)

Markets.disposal — Method
disposal(; product, cost, regions, name)

A process that consumes a product and yields nothing: the way to give a by-product somewhere to go.

Material balances here are equalities, so every unit produced must be used, traded or consumed. A by-product nobody wants is then a bad, and its equilibrium price is negative — the amount somebody must be paid to take it. That is the right answer to the question as posed, but it is rarely the question meant, and under a large supply shock it can drag a local market somewhere surprising.

Adding a disposal route restores the usual assumption. With cost = 0 the price of the product settles at zero rather than going negative, which is free disposal; with a positive cost it settles at minus that cost, which is disposal that has to be paid for — a landfill charge, an incinerator, a tailings dam.

Example

processes = [refinery, steel_mill,
             disposal(product = "fuel_oil"),           # free disposal
             disposal(product = "slag", cost = 12)]    # paid disposal
source
Markets.exogenous_shift — Method
exogenous_shift(; drivers...)

A log-linear shifter of a supply or demand curve, built from the exogenous drivers that move it and the elasticity of the curve with respect to each:

\[\text{shift} \;=\; \prod_j \left(\frac{Z_j}{Z_j^0}\right)^{\gamma_j}\]

Each driver is given as a NamedTuple with its current level, its reference level — the one the curve was calibrated at, which gives a shift of 1 — and the elasticity of the curve with respect to it. Pass them as keyword arguments, named for readability only.

This is the form that comes out of the usual estimation. Regressing $\log S$ on $\log P$ and on the $\log Z_j$ gives the price elasticity and the $\gamma_j$ in one go, so the shifters cost nothing extra to obtain once the curve itself has been estimated.

Example

# a cod stock 18% larger than at calibration, and a slightly larger fleet
shift = exogenous_shift(
    stock = (level = 118.0, reference = 100.0, elasticity = 0.6),
    fleet = (level = 1.05,  reference = 1.0,   elasticity = 0.25))

SupplySpec(product = "cod", region = "EU", p0 = 3000, q0 = 300,
           elasticity = 0.40, shift = shift)

Markets.for_region — Method

Markets.jump_model — Method
jump_model(d::MarketData; kwargs...) -> JuMP.Model

The JuMP model that solve_market would solve for the economy d, built but not solved: print(jump_model(d)) shows its variables, objective and constraints in full, and latex_formulation(jump_model(d)) the same in LaTeX.

Signs in the LaTeX

JuMP 1.32 drops the minus sign of a negative coefficient written in scientific notation when it typesets a model (JuMP.jl#4268), and the benefit terms of an inelastic demand are such coefficients. Read the signs from print until that is fixed.

d is validated first, as solve_market does, and the keywords are those of solve_market that shape the model — solver, solver_options, silent and qfloor — so the model comes back with its solver attached and can be modified and solved with JuMP directly.

An ad valorem tax on trade is not in the objective: it is a fraction of a price, which is a dual of the problem, and solve_market finds its money equivalent by solving repeatedly. The model here is the first of those solves, with those tariffs at zero. Every other tax is in it as it will be solved.

Markets.solve_market — Method
solve_market(
    d::MarketData;
    max_tax_iterations,
    tax_tolerance,
    on_failure,
    kwargs...
) -> Results

Build and solve the equilibrium for the economy d and return a Results.

If the solver does not report an optimal (or locally optimal) solution, on_failure decides what happens: "error" (the default) throws, "warn" warns and "ignore" says nothing. The status, the solver's own message, the time spent and the iterations taken are on the Results.

  • solver is any JuMP solver able to handle nonlinear constraints and to return duals (Ipopt by default), and silent suppresses its output.
  • solver_options are passed on to it, as a NamedTuple, a Dict or a vector of pairs: (max_iter = 500, tol = 1e-9), or Dict("max_iter" => 500, MOI.TimeLimitSec() => 60.0). A name is the solver's own name for the option, and an MOI attribute is passed as it is. They are set after Markets' own settings, and so override them.
  • qfloor is the lower bound given to the quantities that are raised to a power below 1, which an interior-point solver cannot differentiate at zero.
  • tol is the quantity below which a flow, an output or an activity is treated as zero and left out of the result tables.

Both default to a multiple of the smallest reference quantity in d, so that they scale with the units the economy is written in.

An ad valorem tax on trade is a fraction of the price at the origin, which is a dual of the problem and so cannot be written into its objective. When d has one, the equilibrium is found by repeated solves: each holds the tariff at the money equivalent implied by the previous solution's producer prices, and the loop stops when the two agree to tax_tolerance (relative), or warns after max_tax_iterations. Everything else is solved exactly, once.

That iteration is successive substitution, which converges only where the map contracts — quickly in every case tried here, including rates of several hundred per cent, but without a guarantee. The step is therefore halved whenever an iteration increases the error rather than reducing it, which rescues an oscillating case and leaves a well-behaved one at full speed.

Markets.validate — Method
validate(d::MarketData)

Check that an economy refers only to names it declares, and that nothing in it is declared twice. Throws an ArgumentError naming the offending entry on the first problem found; returns nothing when the economy is well formed.

solve_market calls it before building anything, so it rarely has to be called directly. The checks are:

  • regions and products are non-empty and free of duplicates;
  • every product and region named anywhere — in a curve, a process, a transport route, an Armington specification, a TradeLimit or a Tax — is one of those declared;
  • no two SupplySpecs and no two DemandSpecs describe the same product in the same region, and no two Processes share a name;
  • a transport route goes between two different regions;
  • a selector of a tax or a trade limit matches something;
  • no process produces out of nothing at no cost, which would make the economy unbounded.
