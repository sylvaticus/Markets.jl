Using the module

Using Markets.jl takes three steps:

  1. Describe the economy with the data types DemandSpec, SupplySpec, Process (built from leontief and ces nests), collected in a MarketData.
  2. Solve it with solve_market.
  3. Read the results from the DataFrames in the returned Results.

The engine never hard-codes a product, process or region name: everything specific to a sector lives in the data.

All the types and builders below take keyword arguments only, so every number in a model description says what it is. Optional arguments have defaults; the others must be given.

Describing an economy

Regions and products

Regions and products are named with strings:

regions  = ["EU", "US", "CN"]
products = ["crude", "gasoline", "diesel", "fuel_oil", "scrap", "pig_iron", "steel"]

A name may be written as a Symbol instead — :EU is accepted wherever "EU" is — but it is converted on the way in, so what is stored, compared and reported back is always the string. The two spellings may be mixed within one economy, and a field read off a specification compares to the string that built it:

SupplySpec(product = :crude, region = :EU, p0 = 550, q0 = 60, elasticity = 0.4).region == "EU"

Every product gets a material balance in every region, whether or not it is produced, consumed or traded there.

Final demand

A DemandSpec gives a constant-elasticity demand curve for one product in one region, calibrated to pass through a reference point: at price p0 the quantity demanded is q0. elasticity is the own-price elasticity $\eta$, which need only be positive — inelastic demand, $\eta < 1$, is allowed and is what most fuels, foods and basic materials call for:

DemandSpec(product = "gasoline", region = "EU", p0 = 900, q0 = 80, elasticity = 0.3)

With $\eta \le 1$ the objective value is no longer an absolute measure of welfare, only a basis for comparing scenarios; quantities and prices are unaffected. See Demand and supply curves.

Only products with a DemandSpec in a region are consumed there; other products are intermediates in that region.

Products that substitute for each other

Give product a DemandNest instead of a single name and the curve describes a group of substitutes: the p0, q0 and elasticity then belong to the composite, while the nest says how its members trade off against each other.

DemandSpec(region = "EU", p0 = 30000, q0 = 12, elasticity = 0.5,
           product = DemandNest(sigma = 1.6, products = ["electric_car"], shares = [0.25],
                       nests = [DemandNest(sigma = 5.0, share = 0.75,
                                           products = ["petrol_car", "diesel_car"],
                                           shares   = [0.65, 0.35])]))

Petrol and diesel cars substitute readily here, electric cars less so with either.

The data seldom come in that form: they give a price and a quantity for each product, not a share for each and a reference point for the bundle. ces_calibration converts the one into the other, so that at the base-year prices the model buys exactly the base-year quantities:

fuel = ces_calibration(quantities = [7.0, 1.4], prices = [55.0, 250.0], sigma = 3)
DemandSpec(region = "FR", p0 = fuel.price, q0 = fuel.quantity, elasticity = 0.35,
           product = DemandNest(sigma = 3, products = ["firewood", "pellets"],
                                shares = fuel.shares))

fuel.shares are the shares at equal prices, fuel.price is the CES price index of the bundle and fuel.quantity the base-year spending divided by it. A nested bundle is calibrated from the inside out: the price and quantity of an inner group make it one member of the level above. The forest example does this for wood in construction. The structure is a tree for the same reason the Armington one is: a free matrix of cross-price elasticities is not the gradient of any benefit function, and the model would stop being the market it claims to be. See Substitution in final demand.

Substitutes outside the model — public transport against cars — need no nest at all. An exogenous price is a constant, so put the cross-price elasticity into shift through exogenous_shift.

Everything that moves demand other than its own price — income, population, the price of a substitute outside the model — goes in shift, which scales the whole curve and leaves p0 and q0 holding the calibration:

DemandSpec(product = "electricity", region = "EU", p0 = 90, q0 = 2700, elasticity = 0.3,
           shift = exogenous_shift(income = (level = 112.0, reference = 100.0,
                                             elasticity = 0.8)))

Primary supply

A SupplySpec gives a constant-elasticity supply curve for a product that enters the economy from outside the modelled processes — crude oil from a field, ore from a mine, fish from the sea, crops from land:

SupplySpec(product = "crude", region = "US", p0 = 550, q0 = 600, elasticity = 0.4)

A further field, shift, carries the conditions of production: it scales the curve, exactly as for demand. The size of the resource, the roads and ports, the workers and machines available all belong here, and exogenous_shift turns driver levels and their elasticities into the multiplier. A hard ceiling that no price can pass — a fishing quota, a mining licence, an exhausted resource — is a Limit on the supply instead: it truncates the curve rather than scaling it, and the price then rises above the marginal cost on the curve by the scarcity rent of the resource.

supply = [SupplySpec(product = "cod", region = "EU", p0 = 3000, q0 = 300, elasticity = 0.40,
                     shift = exogenous_shift(stock = (level = 118.0, reference = 100.0,
                                                      elasticity = 0.6)))]
limits = [@limit "cod quota" supply(region = "EU", product = "cod") <= 350]

Which of the two to use for which driver, and when to model the factors explicitly instead, is set out in Everything other than the price.

Straight lines and flat curves

Demand and supply curves have constant elasticity by default. Either can be a straight line instead, given in one of two ways:

# through a reference point, with the elasticity there: the tangent line
DemandSpec(product = "wheat", region = "EG", p0 = 88, q0 = 7634, elasticity = 0.55,
           form = :linear)
# by its coefficients, D = alpha + beta·p and S = alpha + beta·p
DemandSpec(product = "wheat", region = "A", alpha = 200, beta = -9)
SupplySpec(product = "wheat", region = "A", alpha = -50, beta = 10.5)

The coefficients carry their signs as a regression would estimate them: beta < 0 for demand, beta > 0 for supply, and for demand alpha > 0, the quantity demanded at a zero price. A supply alpha may have either sign: negative, nothing is supplied below the price -alpha/beta. Giving both a reference point and coefficients is an error, as is giving neither.

In either form, shift multiplies the quantity at every price, and exogenous_shift builds it from drivers as before. On a line it scales alpha and beta together, so the price at which the line meets zero stays put. A line estimated in levels, Q = alpha + beta·p + gamma·Z, carries its drivers in the intercept instead: give the moved line its new alpha. The calibration helpers (ces_calibration, armington_calibration) return a reference point, which serves either form.

A scenario moves a curve by giving it a new shifter, whatever its form:

salvage = SupplySpec(logs; shift = 3.0)     # the same curve, three times the quantity

An elasticity of Inf makes the curve flat, in either form: a price given from outside, at which any quantity is bought or sold. It is how a small country meets the world market, the region "world" selling at the import price and buying at the export price:

supply = [SupplySpec(product = "wheat", region = "world", p0 = 96.8, q0 = 1, elasticity = Inf)]
demand = [DemandSpec(product = "wheat", region = "world", p0 = 88.0, q0 = 1, elasticity = Inf)]

q0 plays no role on a flat curve but must still be positive, and a flat curve refuses any shift but 1: there is no quantity to scale, and moving it means changing p0. The import price has to be above the export price, transport included, or buying from the world to sell back to it would be a profit without end, and the market has no answer.

Processes and input nests

A Process turns inputs into outputs at a level of activity $z$ chosen by the model. It has:

  • a list of input nests. Each nest is one input requirement per unit of activity. Different nests are needed in fixed proportion to each other; products inside the same nest substitute for each other.
  • a list of outputs, as product => yield pairs per unit of activity. Several outputs make a joint-production process (a main product and its residues).
  • a value-added cost vacost per unit of activity, covering everything not modelled as an explicit input (labour, energy, capital, ...) — a single number for every region, or a region => cost dictionary covering each region the process operates in.
  • optionally the regions where it may operate (default: all).

The installed capacity of the plants is a Limit on the activity, region by region:

limits = [@limit("steel plants $r", activity(region = r, process = "steel_mill") <= cap)
          for (r, cap) in installed]

Without one, a process with constant returns concentrates wherever production is cheapest; with one, the bound binds and earns a quasi-rent, reported in Results.activity as well as in Results.limits.

There are two kinds of nest:

  • leontief: exactly coeff units of a single product per unit of activity.
  • ces: composite units of a CES bundle of several products. shares are the value shares of each input when all input prices are equal (equal shares if omitted; ces_calibration derives them from observed input quantities and prices), and sigma (≥ 0) is the elasticity of substitution: the higher it is, the more the mix moves away from the expensive input when relative prices change. sigma = 0 needs the inputs in fixed proportions, sigma = 1 is Cobb–Douglas, and sigma = Inf makes them perfect substitutes.

For example, a refinery that splits each tonne of crude into 0.45 t of petrol, 0.35 t of diesel and 0.15 t of fuel oil, and a steel mill that needs 1.1 t of a substitutable mix of scrap and pig iron per tonne of steel:

Process(name    = "refinery",
        inputs  = [leontief(product = "crude", coeff = 1.0)],
        outputs = ["gasoline" => 0.45, "diesel" => 0.35, "fuel_oil" => 0.15],
        vacost  = 40)

Process(name    = "steel_mill",
        inputs  = [ces(composite = 1.10,
                       products  = ["scrap", "pig_iron"],
                       shares    = [0.4, 0.6],
                       sigma     = 2.0)],
        outputs = ["steel" => 1.0],
        vacost  = 150)

A process with two nests, e.g.

inputs = [ces(composite = 1.0, products = ["a", "b"], shares = [0.5, 0.5], sigma = 2),
          leontief(product = "c", coeff = 0.2)]

needs one unit of the $a$/$b$ bundle and 0.2 units of $c$ per unit of activity.

Trade

transport_costs maps each allowed route (product, from, to) to a unit transport cost, per unit of quantity shipped. It is also the whole declaration of trade: a route missing from it cannot be used, and a product with no route anywhere is simply not traded, so there is no separate list of tradable products to keep in step with it. Routes are directional, which is what lets a corridor cost more one way than the other, so a product that moves both ways needs an entry each way.

transport_costs = Dict(("crude", "US", "EU") => 25.0, ("crude", "EU", "US") => 25.0, ...)

A free route is written => 0.0, not omitted.

What may actually move is capped by a Limit on the flows: the capacity of a port or a corridor, or a quota. It can cap a single product on a single route or everything crossing a border, and weights convert the quantities into whatever unit the ceiling is written in:

limits = [@limit("steel quota", flow(product = "steel", from = "CN", to = "EU") <= 8.0),
          @limit("container port", 2.0 * exports(region = "CN", product = "cars") +
                                   0.04 * exports(region = "CN", product = "wine") <= 6.0)]

A binding ceiling shows up in Results.limits with its congestion rent, which every flow it covers carries in Results.trade, and the price gap across the route it throttles becomes transport plus that rent.

By default a traded product is homogeneous: the same good wherever it comes from. Regional prices then differ by at most the transport cost and no region imports and exports it at once.

Imperfect substitution between origins (optional)

Listing a product in Armington makes the varieties of the different origins imperfect substitutes instead: each region uses a CES composite of them, so it can import and export the same product at once and its price responds to supply and demand everywhere rather than only to the cheapest source.

armington = [Armington(product = "wine", sigma = 4),              # equal shares
             Armington(product = "cars", sigma = 6,
                       shares = Dict(("EU", "EU") => 0.7, ("US", "EU") => 0.2, ("CN", "EU") => 0.1,
                                     ("US", "US") => 0.8, ("EU", "US") => 0.1, ("CN", "US") => 0.1,
                                     ("CN", "CN") => 0.9, ("EU", "CN") => 0.05, ("US", "CN") => 0.05))]

  • sigma is the elasticity of substitution between origins, any value ≥ 0: the lower it is, the more buyers stick to their usual origin when prices move. Below 1 the varieties are poor substitutes, or complements; sigma = 1 is Cobb–Douglas (each origin keeps a fixed share of the spending) and sigma = 0 fixes the mix of origins whatever the prices.

  • shares are keyed (origin, destination) and are the shares that would be observed if all delivered prices were equal, so they carry the home bias. They are not the observed shares: calibrate them from a base-year trade matrix with armington_shares, which inverts the CES demand condition for you — it is ces_calibration applied to each destination.

    shares = armington_shares(flows = base_year_quantities,   # (origin, dest) => quantity
                          prices = delivered_prices,      # (origin, dest) => price
                          sigma = 4)

    If omitted, every origin available to a destination gets an equal share. An origin with no share (or a share of 0) is left out of that destination's composite, and so can never gain trade later.

    With origin groups (below), armington_calibration builds the whole specification from the same two dictionaries — shares through every level of the tree, and the scale that keeps each composite in the physical units of the flows at the base year, so that base-year data balance exactly. Where flows are missing — between the regions of one country, typically — impute_flows fills them in from production, use and whatever totals are known; see Calibrating with partial data.

  • sigma = Inf, and any product not listed, is the homogeneous case above. Inside an OriginNest it instead pools that group of origins into a single good, which is how a set of regions that form one internal market — the regions of a country, say — enters a world model as one variety.

With an Armington product, local producers and local users no longer face the same price, and both are reported — see Reading the results.

Shares or elasticity?

Use the shares to say how much of an origin a market takes when prices are equal — that is where a home bias, or a regulatory penalty against an origin, belongs. Use sigma to say how readily buyers switch when prices move. A low sigma is not a barrier: it means buyers cannot get away from that origin, so an expensive one keeps its share rather than losing it.

Origins that are closer substitutes than others

A single sigma makes every origin substitute equally well for every other. When some origins are near-interchangeable — shared grades, standards or certification — and others are not, group them in an OriginNest with its own, higher elasticity:

armington = [Armington(product = "cars", sigma = 2.5,
                       nests = [OriginNest(sigma = 12, origins = ["EU", "US"])],
                       shares = shares)]

Buyers then swap EU for US cars readily (σ = 12) and replace either with Chinese cars only slowly (σ = 2.5). Origins left out of every group are direct members of the composite, groups may contain groups, and a group must be at least as substitutable inside as it is with the outside — the engine rejects the reverse, which would be inconsistent.

Different structures in different markets

Standards and non-tariff measures are set by the importer, so they are asymmetric. Give a specification a destination to apply it to those markets only, and keep one with destination = : (every destination, the default) as the fallback:

armington = [
    # the EU market keeps Chinese cars at arm's length, EU and US are alike
    Armington(product = "cars", destination = "EU", sigma = 2.5,
              nests = [OriginNest(sigma = 12, origins = ["EU", "US"])], shares = shares),
    # every other market substitutes freely between all three origins
    Armington(product = "cars", sigma = 8, shares = shares)]

The elasticity, the groups and the shares may all differ by destination. What may not differ is whether the product is Armington at all: it has one variety per origin everywhere, or none anywhere.

Putting it together

market = MarketData(regions   = regions,
                    products  = products,
                    demand    = demand,
                    supply    = supply,
                    processes = processes,
                    transport_costs = transport_costs)

Only regions and products are required; demand, supply, processes and transport_costs all default to empty. When your variables already carry the field names, Julia's shorthand saves the repetition:

market = MarketData(; regions, products, demand, supply, processes, transport_costs)

Taxes and subsidies

A Tax drives a wedge between what one side pays and what the other receives, on production, on consumption, on a transformation or on a trade route. rate is an amount per unit, or a fraction of the base when relative = true; a subsidy is a negative rate.

taxes = [Tax(name = "oil_royalty",  on = "supply",  product = "crude", region = "US", rate = 4.0),
         Tax(name = "fuel_vat",     on = "demand",  product = "gasoline", rate = 0.2,
             relative = true),
         Tax(name = "green_steel",  on = "process", process = "steel_mill", region = "EU",
             rate = -15.0),
         Tax(name = "steel_tariff", on = "trade",   product = "steel", to = "US", rate = 0.25,
             relative = true)]

Selectors left at : — the colon, the default — match everything, and several taxes may fall on one base. Results.taxes reports what each raises — which matters, because a taxed objective counts the revenue as a cost rather than a transfer and so is no longer welfare. See Taxes, subsidies and other wedges.

Limits on any quantity

A Limit is a linear constraint on the quantities of the model: a resource that several products share, a quota, a minimum, a share of one quantity in another, a fixed sum. @limit writes it as the inequality it stands for:

limits = [@limit("stands of SEF", supply(region = "SEF", product = "logs") +
                                  supply(region = "SEF", product = "firewood") <= 12.4),
          @limit("milled at home", activity(region = "SWF", process = "sawmill") >=
                                   0.5 * supply(region = "SWF", product = "logs")),
          @limit("import share", imports(region = "OF", product = "lumber") <=
                                 consumption(region = "OF", product = "lumber") / 3),
          @limit("export band", 1.0 <= exports(region = "RW", product = "logs") <= 3.0),
          @limit("fixed quota", flow(product = "logs", from = "RW", to = "EU") == 2.0)]

market = MarketData(; regions, products, demand, supply, processes, transport_costs, limits)

The quantities are named by eight term builders, each summing over the regions, products and processes it selects — a name, a vector of names, or the colon : for every one, which is also what a selector left out means:

BuilderSums
supply(; region, product)primary supply
consumption(; region, product)final demand
production(; region, product)primary supply plus process outputs
activity(; region, process)process activity levels
inputs(; region, process, product)what processes draw of their inputs
flow(; product, from, to)shipments between regions
exports(; region, product)shipments leaving the region, or the group of regions
imports(; region, product)shipments entering it

Quantities may be added, subtracted, multiplied or divided by numbers, put on either side of the comparison, and mixed with constants, which end up in the bound. lower <= expression <= upper sets both bounds at once, and == fixes the expression. Anything else — the product of two quantities, a quantity in a denominator, a power, sqrt — is refused when the limit is written, because it would no longer be linear: a linear limit keeps the problem convex, so the equilibrium is still the solution of one optimisation and its rent has a clear meaning.

A selector is the same everywhere in the package — in a Tax, in a Process's regions, in an Armington destination — and no name in it is special: a region or a product may be called all and is then selected by that name. Every one is :, as in indexing an array:

@limit "world softwood" supply(region = :, product = "swr") <= 900

A group of regions or products is a vector you build yourself — france = ["SEF", "SWF", "GEF", "OF"] — and supply(region = france, product = "swr") sums over its members. For a group, exports and imports count only what crosses its border, while flow counts every flow between the regions selected.

Families of limits

Where GAMS has indexed equations and JuMP families of constraints, @limits writes one limit per combination of its indices, each named after them:

@limits "cut" [r in france] supply(region = r, product = "swr") <= cap[r]
## cut[SEF], cut[SWF], cut[GEF], cut[OF]

@limits "stands" [r in regions, p in primary; haskey(stock, (r, p))] supply(region = r, product = p) <= stock[(r, p)]
## stands[SEF, swr], stands[SEF, hwr], …, only for the pairs with a stock

An index is written i in I, i ∈ I or i = I; a condition after a semicolon keeps the combinations that meet it, and the last index varies fastest. The result is a plain vector of limits, so it is concatenated with others like any vector. A comprehension does the same with names of your choosing:

[@limit("cut $r", supply(region = r, product = "swr") <= cap[r]) for r in france]

To read a family back from the results, select its members by name: res.limits[startswith.(res.limits.limit, "cut["), :].

Soft limits

A limit is hard: no price buys past it. Followed by penalty = p, it becomes soft, and can be crossed at a cost of p per unit beyond the bound — the way a sector model holds trade near its recent levels without forbidding a larger move when the market really calls for it:

@limit "pulp imports DE" imports(region = "DE", product = "pulp") <= 1.1 * last_year penalty = 50
@limits "trade" [p in products, r in regions] imports(region = r, product = p) <= 1.1 * last[(r, p)] penalty = 50

Below the bound a soft limit changes nothing. Past it, its rent is the penalty, which is the most a soft limit's rent can be, and Results.limits says by how much it was crossed in excess. A lower bound works the same way, on the shortfall. The problem stays convex: the crossing is a variable with a linear cost.

The penalty is a device of the model, not a cost anyone pays. The results report its total in penalties, and welfare leaves it out. A cost that is really paid past a threshold — overtime past a shift, an emergency import past a contract — belongs in the economy instead, as a second process or route at the higher cost. A soft limit on one variable is not a capacity, since it can be exceeded, so the capacity columns ignore it.

Writing limits by hand

Inside @limit the builders are those of Markets.jl, so a variable of your own called supply is not in the way; outside it, write them Markets.supply(…) and so on, and give the expression to Limit yourself:

Limit(name = "stands of SEF", upper = 12.4,
      expression = Markets.supply(region = "SEF", product = ["logs", "firewood"]))

Two cautions. A macro reads everything up to the end of the line, so several in one call need parentheses, as above. And consumption and inputs of an Armington product are measured, as the model measures them, in units of the region's composite, while flow, exports and imports are physical; a share that mixes the two is linear and valid, but slightly off physically (see Physical accounts).

Limits are generated like any other data, in loops. A resource that several products share — stands that give both logs and firewood, say — is kept from being counted twice by bounding every combination of the products by the stock of everything at least one of them can come from, which is exactly what makes the harvests fit into the stock (Hall's theorem):

combinations(xs) = [xs[[isodd(i >> (j - 1)) for j in eachindex(xs)]] for i in 1:(2^length(xs) - 1)]
limits = [@limit("$r: " * join(C, " + "),
                 sum(supply(region = r, product = p) for p in C) <= available(r, C))
          for r in regions for C in combinations(primary)]

Results.limits reports each limit with its bounds, the value its expression takes, its rent — the gain in the objective from relaxing the bound that binds by one unit, positive for an upper bound and negative for a lower one, zero for a limit that does not bind — and the regions and model variables it applies to, which Markets.scope(limit, economy) also gives before anything is solved. The rent reaches the quantities too: the supply, activity and trade tables give each row the rent the limits it is part of charge per unit of it — the whole rent for a ceiling on that quantity alone, the rent times its weight for a share of a wider one — and the capacity of the tightest limit on that quantity alone. And for_region slices Results.limits too, keeping the limits that apply to the region, alone or with others.

A limit that bounds one variable from above — a single supply, activity or flow — is set as a bound on that variable rather than as a constraint, which costs the solver nothing; any other limit is a constraint of the model. The forest example uses it for its stands.

Looking at an economy

Typing the name of a MarketData in the REPL gives one line, such as MarketData(3 regions, 7 products, 4 processes). Printing it, with print(market), gives a summary: what is supplied, consumed, made and used of each product, the processes in the order they chain into one another (stage 1 uses only primary products, stage 2 also the outputs of stage 1, and so on), the trade routes and their costs, the Armington specifications, the trade limits and the taxes and subsidies. For a steel chain that ends in cars:

Transformations, by stage
  1  blast_furnace  1.6 iron_ore + 0.8 coal ───────→ pig_iron + 0.3 slag  cost 60
  2  steel_mill     1.1 CES(scrap, pig_iron; σ 2) ─→ steel                cost 150
  2  dispose_slag   slag ──────────────────────────→ (disposed of)        cost 5
  3  car_plant      0.9 steel ─────────────────────→ cars                 cost 20000 · cap EU 14, US 13, CN 30

The mathematical program the economy becomes is returned, built but not solved, by jump_model:

m = jump_model(market)      # the JuMP model solve_market would solve
print(m)                    # objective, balances, bounds, in full
latex_formulation(m)        # the same, in LaTeX

latex_formulation is JuMP's, re-exported so that it needs no using JuMP. In a notebook or on a documentation page it is typeset; in a terminal it prints the LaTeX source.

Check the signs against `print`

JuMP 1.32 drops the minus sign of a negative coefficient written in scientific notation when it typesets a model (JuMP.jl#4268). The benefit term of a demand with $\eta < 1$ has a negative coefficient, usually a large one, so in most models some objective terms are shown with the wrong sign. print(m) is not affected; read the signs from it until the bug is fixed.

Its variables and constraints are named after what they stand for — D[EU,steel], S[US,crude], z[EU,steel_mill], balance[EU,steel], limit[container port] — and it takes the same solver, solver_options, silent and qfloor keywords as solve_market, so it can be modified and solved with JuMP directly. An ad valorem tax on trade is the one thing missing from it: solve_market finds its money equivalent by solving repeatedly, and the model returned is the first of those solves, with the tax at zero.

Solving

res = solve_market(market)                       # Ipopt, silent
res = solve_market(market; silent = false)       # show the Ipopt log
res = solve_market(market; solver = MyOpt)       # any JuMP nonlinear solver
res = solve_market(market; solver_options = (max_iter = 500, tol = 1e-9))

solver_options takes a NamedTuple, a Dict or a vector of pairs. A name is the solver's own name for the option, written as a symbol or a string, and an MOI attribute such as MOI.TimeLimitSec() is passed as it is. The options are set after the ones Markets chooses for Ipopt (see Numerical details), so they override them.

Before building anything, solve_market calls validate on the economy. That check is not a formality: the model has one balance per declared region and product, so a name outside those lists would get variables and no balance — a mistyped process input would become a free input and the solver would report success. Every name, duplicate and selector is therefore checked first, and a problem is an ArgumentError saying which entry is at fault.

If the solver then returns anything but an optimal or locally optimal solution, solve_market throws. An infeasible or unbounded economy comes back with tables that look exactly like those of a solved one, so returning them quietly is the more dangerous default. Pass on_failure = "warn" or "ignore" to get them anyway, and read res.status to see what happened.

Reading the results

A Results carries the solver's verdict in status and its own message in raw_status, the solve_time in seconds and the iterations it took (summed over all solves when an ad valorem tariff is found by iteration, and missing for a solver that does not count them), the value of the maximised objective, the welfare that objective implies once tax revenue is counted as the transfer it is and the penalties of soft limits are taken back out, and twelve DataFrames:

Either price column is missing where it has no meaning: a region that cannot produce an Armington product has no producer_price for it, and a region that uses none of one has no price. Prices are signed, so a by-product that must be disposed of at a cost shows a negative one.

price and producer_price differ only for an Armington product, where local users buy a composite of all origins' varieties while local producers sell their own; for every other product the two columns are equal.

quantity and physical differ only for an Armington product: its users draw on a CES composite of the varieties, which equals their physical sum only at the mix it was calibrated at. The last three tables keep the physical account of the economy, and gap is the quantity the CES aggregation used up or made up; see Physical accounts.

for_region slices all tables for one region at once. The input data and the underlying JuMP model are kept in res.data and res.model.

Tracing a material

What the goods are made of — carbon, dry matter, a metal — is traced through a solved economy by material_accounts, given a Material: its content per unit of each primary product, the share each process loses, and, for a process with several outputs, how it divides the rest among them.

carbon = Material(name = "carbon",
                  content = Dict("logs" => 0.25),                  # per m³ harvested
                  losses  = Dict("sawmill" => 0.15, "pulpmill" => 0.5),
                  allocation = Dict("sawmill" => Dict("lumber" => 1.0, "chips" => 1.0)))
acc = material_accounts(res, carbon)
acc.intensities   # content per unit of each region's production, and of what it uses
acc.balances      # extracted, made, imported, exported, consumed, processed
acc.processes     # what enters each process, what it loses, what it passes on

The content of a manufactured good is not given but follows from the solution, so the accounts close exactly, region by region and for the world: what was extracted was consumed or lost. The consumed column is each region's footprint. The accounts describe the equilibrium and do not change it; see Material accounts.

Worked examples

Two regions and a dairy is the smallest complete model — two products, one process, one route, and milk that is both drunk and turned into cheese — and the place to start.

The forest example builds a complete economy with this API, over three pages. The first writes the model — eight regions (four of them French), the roundwood-to-paper-and-pellets chain, a product that is both burnt directly and milled, joint products, CES input bundles, a process restricted to the regions that have the capacity, and a three-level Armington structure in which the French regions are near-perfect substitutes for each other, close substitutes for the rest of the EU and more distant ones for the other blocs — and runs three scenarios on it, with uncalibrated numbers: a storm that floods one region with salvaged timber, a dearer gas, and a third of the French forests set aside. The second builds the same model from partial, inconsistent statistics read from CSV files, calibrating every parameter it does not observe or choose; the third from complete, consistent data, and compares the scenarios of the three.

The pages are generated from the scripts in examples/forest/, which are ordinary scripts: run them, in order, to get the same tables in a terminal.

julia --project=docs examples/forest/forest_market.jl

Two more pages port models published with their code, from the GAMS model library, and check each port against the original. Takayama and Judge is the textbook spatial equilibrium with straight-line supply and demand, and Egyptian agriculture a sector model of 1980: crops as processes, land, water and labour by month as limits, livestock fed from fodder, and a small country trading at world prices. Its port is compared line by line with a JuMP transcription of the original.

How to amend an economy

  • Add a product: add its name to products; give it a DemandSpec and/or SupplySpec where it is consumed or produced; wire it into processes; add transport_costs entries if it can be shipped.
  • Add or change a process: add a Process. Chains of any length are built by making the output of one process an input of another (e.g. iron ore → steel → cars). A product can be primary-supplied, manufactured, consumed as final demand and used as an input all at once — its balance simply sums every source against every use.
  • Add a region: add it to regions and give it demand, supply and transport data.
  • Change substitutability between inputs: change the sigma of a CES nest. Values near 1 give a near Cobb–Douglas mix; large values make the inputs close to perfect substitutes.
  • Change substitutability between origins: add or amend an Armington entry. Leave a product out of armington (or give it sigma = Inf) for the homogeneous, spatial-equilibrium treatment.

No change to the package code is needed for any of these.
