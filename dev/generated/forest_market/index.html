The forest sector: France in the world

This example builds a complete spatial partial-equilibrium model of the forest-products sector with Markets.jl, solves it, and runs three scenarios on it. It is the first of three pages:

  1. this one, on how to write the model and use it, with the kind of numbers that are at hand before any calibration effort;
  2. Calibrating with partial data, on turning incomplete, inconsistent statistics into the same model;
  3. Complete data, and what calibration changes, on what perfect information would take, and how much the scenarios depend on which of the three you have.
For illustrative purposes only

The data in this example are plausible, but they are not statistics, and are not calibrated: what that means, and why it matters, is the subject of the last section. For simplicity, the structure of the model and its data are also written together here; the next pages read the data from files, as an application should.

The economy has eight regions. France is split into four regions and the rest of the world into four blocs:

SEFSouth-east France — the Alps and the Massif Central, spruce and fir
SWFSouth-west France — the Landes, Europe's largest maritime-pine plantation
GEFGrand Est — the Vosges, and the oak and beech of the north-east
OFthe rest of France
EUthe rest of the European Union
NANorth America
ASAsia-Pacific
RWthe rest of the world

What makes the regional detail work is the way the varieties of a product substitute for each other, which is the subject of the Armington section below: French products are near-perfect substitutes for each other, close substitutes for the rest of the EU, and more distant substitutes for the other blocs.

The sector modelled here is the one the Global Forest Products Model established as the reference, Buongiorno et al. (2003); for the same economics in working open-source code, with different choices about dynamics and data, see TiMBA, [C3].

Quantities are in million m³ for wood products and million tonnes for pulp, paper and pellets; prices are in USD per m³ or per tonne.

using Markets, DataFrames

Regions and products

france  = ["SEF", "SWF", "GEF", "OF"]
regions = [france..., "EU", "NA", "AS", "RW"]

The product list follows the FAOSTAT / UNECE chain, splitting roundwood by coniferous (softwood, swr) and non-coniferous (hardwood, hwr), because the two lead to different products.

smallwood is the third assortment the forest yields: small-diameter roundwood from thinnings and tops, too poor for sawing. In French statistics it is the bois d'industrie et bois énergie category, and the name says what matters here — the same log can be burnt in a stove or sent to a mill, and which it is depends on what the two uses are willing to pay.

products = ["swr", "hwr", "smallwood", "chips", "pulp",
            "sawn_sw", "sawn_hw", "panel", "plywood", "paper", "pellets"]

They are connected by the transformations set up further down:

  FOREST ── swr ───────┬─ sawmill_sw ─→ sawn_sw + chips
         ── hwr ───────┼─ sawmill_hw ─→ sawn_hw + chips
         ── smallwood ─┤
                       ├─ plymill    : CES(swr, hwr) ───────────────────→ plywood + chips
                       ├─ panelmill  : CES(chips, smallwood, swr, hwr) ─→ panel
                       ├─ pulpmill   : CES(swr, hwr, chips, smallwood) ─→ pulp ─→ papermill ─→ paper
                       └─ pelletmill : CES(chips, smallwood, swr, hwr) ─→ pellets

                          smallwood ─────────────────────────────────→ burnt as firewood

Two things in that picture are worth more than a passing glance.

chips are a sawmill and plymill by-product and an input to three other mills, so the competition for residues is part of the equilibrium rather than an assumption about it.

smallwood is both a final product and an intermediate one. It comes out of the forest, and from there it either goes into a household stove as firewood — final demand, like sawnwood or paper — or into a mill as a raw material. The model does not need to be told which: both uses face the same local price, and the split between them is whatever that price makes optimal. A product in this engine may be primary-supplied, manufactured, consumed and used as an input all at once; its balance simply sums the sources and the uses.

Primary supply

Roundwood coming out of the forest, as a constant-elasticity curve through a reference point: each region's harvest at its roadside price, which forest statistics give. The French regions harvest some 31 Mm³ of industrial roundwood between them: the Landes (SWF) dominate softwood, while GEF and OF hold most of the hardwood. Their supply elasticities are lower than the blocs' — French forests are fragmented between many small private owners, which makes the harvest slow to respond to price.

Final demand

Only finished products are consumed. Each region's consumption is known, and OF — which holds two thirds of the French population — is much the largest French market. For prices, what is usually at hand is one price per product: a world or national average, the same for every region.

The own-price elasticities are below 1: demand for forest products is usually found to be price-inelastic, construction lumber and paper most of all, since their cost is a small part of the building or the printed product they end up in. An inelastic demand makes prices, rather than quantities, absorb a supply shock — which matters for the storm scenario at the end.

Products that compete for the same job

Those seven products are not seven separate markets. Softwood lumber and panels do the same job in a building and a builder swaps one for the other readily; hardwood lumber is a more distant substitute for either. Pellets and firewood are the same heat in different packaging. Paper competes with nothing else here.

That is a DemandNest each: a tree of products with one demand curve on the bundle. It has to be a tree rather than a matrix of cross-price elasticities, for the reason set out in Substitution in final demand — a free matrix is not the gradient of any benefit function, and the model would stop being the market it claims to represent.

Calibrating a bundle

A demand curve over a bundle needs three numbers that no statistic reports: the shares of its members, and the price and quantity of the bundle itself, which are the p0 and q0 of its curve. What the reference table gives instead is a price and a quantity for each product. ces_calibration turns the second into the first, choosing the three so that at the base-year prices the model buys exactly the base-year quantities. For the wood fuel of OF, 5.05 Mm³ of firewood at 70 and 1.73 Mt of pellets at 285:

ces_calibration(quantities = [5.05, 1.73], prices = [70.0, 285.0], sigma = 3)
(shares = [0.041458696774834, 0.958541303225166], price = 222.15629247442956, quantity = 3.8106055451813896, scale = 1.7792447734648071)
  • The shares are what each product's share would be if both cost the same. Pellets get 96% of the bundle although households buy three times more firewood: they are bought little because they are four times dearer, and would be bought far more at an equal price.
  • The price is the CES price index of the bundle — the cost of one unit of it at the base-year prices.
  • The quantity is the base-year spending, 847, divided by that price. It is an index number, not a volume: m³ of firewood and tonnes of pellets would mean nothing added up, while their values at a common price do. That is what a CES composite is.

Feed those back into the CES demand condition at the base-year prices and out come exactly 5.05 and 1.73.

Wood in buildings

Construction is a nest inside a nest — softwood lumber, particleboard and plywood together at σ = 5, hardwood lumber against that group at σ = 1.6. The three in the inner group are what a builder actually chooses between for a floor or a wall; appearance-grade hardwood is a different kind of purchase.

A nested bundle is calibrated from the inside out. The inner group is calibrated on its three products; its price and quantity then make it one member of the outer bundle, next to hardwood lumber; and the outer price and quantity are the reference point of the curve:

σ_lumber, σ_construction, σ_fuel = 5.0, 1.6, 3.0
lumber = ["sawn_sw", "panel", "plywood"]

function construction_demand(r)
    q(p) = reference[p][2][r]                    # base-year quantity in region r
    price(p) = reference[p][1]                   # base-year price
    inner = ces_calibration(quantities = q.(lumber), prices = price.(lumber),
                            sigma = σ_lumber)
    outer = ces_calibration(quantities = [q("sawn_hw"), inner.quantity],
                            prices     = [price("sawn_hw"), inner.price],
                            sigma = σ_construction)
    return DemandCurve(region = r, p0 = outer.price, q0 = outer.quantity, elasticity = 0.45,
        product = DemandNest(sigma = σ_construction,
            products = ["sawn_hw"], shares = [outer.shares[1]],
            nests = [DemandNest(sigma = σ_lumber, share = outer.shares[2],
                                products = lumber, shares = inner.shares)]))
end
construction_demand (generic function with 1 method)

The elasticity of 0.45 is the own-price elasticity of the whole bundle: how much less wood goes into buildings when all of it gets dearer. How the bundle splits when only one product gets dearer is the job of the two σ.

Wood for heat

Wood fuel is a single nest, and the one place where a price from outside the model matters: firewood and pellets both compete with gas and oil, whose prices this sector does not determine. An exogenous price needs no nest at all — being a constant, it shifts the whole curve — so the cross-price elasticity goes into exogenous_shift. Here gas sits at its reference level, which leaves the shifter at exactly 1; the scenario at the end moves it.

gas_price, gas_reference, gas_cross_elasticity = 30.0, 30.0, 0.35
fuels = ["smallwood", "pellets"]

function fuel_demand(r, gas = gas_price)
    fuel = ces_calibration(quantities = [reference[p][2][r] for p in fuels],
                           prices     = [reference[p][1] for p in fuels], sigma = σ_fuel)
    return DemandCurve(region = r, p0 = fuel.price, q0 = fuel.quantity, elasticity = 0.35,
        shift = exogenous_shift(gas = (level = gas, reference = gas_reference,
                                       elasticity = gas_cross_elasticity)),
        product = DemandNest(sigma = σ_fuel, products = fuels, shares = fuel.shares))
end

All of final demand

Paper competes with nothing here, so it keeps a plain curve through its own reference point. The whole demand side is then one curve per bundle and region. It is built by a function of the gas price rather than written out once, so that the gas scenario at the end can rebuild it at another price:

build_demand(gas = gas_price) =
    [[construction_demand(r) for r in regions];
     [fuel_demand(r, gas) for r in regions];
     [DemandCurve(product = "paper", region = r, p0 = reference["paper"][1],
                 q0 = reference["paper"][2][r], elasticity = 0.30) for r in regions]]

demand = build_demand()

Processes

Six transformations, of three kinds.

Fixed proportions with a by-product. Sawmilling turns a log into lumber and chips in set amounts. The plywood mill is the same shape — veneer is peeled from the log, and what is left, nearly half of it, comes out as chips — except that its input is a CES bundle of the two roundwood grades, because a veneer mill can work softwood or hardwood and will lean toward whichever is cheaper.

A CES bundle of wood. Panel, pulp and pellet mills draw on a mix of chips, smallwood and roundwood, so when chips become scarce — because the other mills are bidding for them — each shifts smoothly toward roundwood.

Plywood sits on both sides of that: it sheds chips, which helps the pulp mill, and bids for the same logs, which does not. Which effect wins is not something to guess at; it is the kind of link a sector model exists to settle.

The recipes below — the shares of each wood in a bundle, the value-added costs — are reasonable guesses, the same in every region. The next page works them out from data instead.

Fixed proportions, single input. The paper mill takes pulp and nothing else.

Pulp mills are capital-heavy and few: in France only the Landes (SWF) and a handful of sites elsewhere (OF) have the capacity, which the regions argument expresses. SEF and GEF therefore have no pulp of their own and must buy it from somewhere else.

Freight is charged per unit of quantity, not of value, because shipping a m³ of logs costs much the same whether the logs are cheap or dear. Bulky, low-value products therefore carry a high transport cost relative to their price, which is what keeps roundwood trade regional and lets paper travel.

Who substitutes for whom

This is where the regional detail earns its place. If the varieties of a product were perfect substitutes everywhere, each market would simply buy from the cheapest source and a small cost advantage would take everything. If instead they were all imperfect substitutes with one elasticity, French oak would be no closer to French pine than to Asian hardwood, which is just as wrong.

What the data say is a hierarchy:

  • inside France the product of one region is nearly the same good as that of another — a m³ of Landes pine and a m³ of Vosges pine of the same grade are all but interchangeable, and mostly transport separates them;
  • between France and the rest of the EU the products are close but not identical: shared standards and certification, short distances, but different species mixes and supply contracts;
  • between the big blocs they are more distant substitutes: different species, grading rules, phytosanitary requirements and commercial habits.

That hierarchy is a tree, which is exactly what nested OriginNests express: the French regions together at a very high elasticity, then France and the rest of the EU at a high one, then Europe as a whole against the other blocs at a lower one.

Commodities graded to a standard (chips, roundwood, pulp) substitute readily between blocs; manufactured products, where origin carries a reputation, do so less. Within Europe every product substitutes three times more easily than between blocs, and within France more easily still:

σ_europe = Dict(p => 3σ for (p, σ) in σ_blocs)
σ_france = 30.0

europe(p) = OriginNest(sigma   = σ_europe[p],
                       origins = ["EU"],
                       nests   = [OriginNest(sigma = σ_france, origins = france)])

Note the direction of the inequality the package enforces: a group must hold together at least as tightly as it holds to the outside, 30 ≥ 3σ ≥ σ. The reverse would say that members of a group are worse substitutes for each other than for outsiders, which no cost function can represent.

Why not make the French regions perfect substitutes, sigma = Inf, and France a single market up to transport? Because with mills that have constant returns, that can leave the model unable to say where in France the milling happens — see Perfect substitutes and constant returns for why, and for the remedies. A finite elasticity, however high, gives each region's product a demand of its own, which settles it.

The shares are the other half of the specification: they say how much of each origin a market would take if all delivered prices were equal, so they carry the home bias and the trade pattern that prices alone do not explain. Calibrating them properly means fitting a base-year trade matrix, which is what the next page does; here they come from a gravity rule — big suppliers and near ones weigh more, and a region leans toward itself.

size_of = Dict(r => sum(s.q0 for s in supply if s.region == r) for r in regions)
decay, home_bias = 1.4, 3.0
shares = Dict((o, r) => size_of[o] * exp(-decay * distance(o, r)) * (o == r ? home_bias : 1.0)
              for o in regions, r in regions)

One Armington specification per product then assembles the three levels. The same structure applies to every destination here, but it need not — a destination argument would let the EU market treat a bloc differently from how that bloc treats the EU, which is what non-tariff measures do. The gravity rule gives every pair a share, but a region that mills no pulp has no pulp variety to offer: the engine would drop such a share, and validate would warn about it, so the pulp shares are kept to the regions that make it.

offers(p) = p == "pulp" ? pulp_regions : regions
armington = [Armington(product = p,
                       sigma   = σ_blocs[p],
                       shares  = Dict(k => v for (k, v) in shares if k[1] in offers(p)),
                       nests   = [europe(p)])
             for p in products]

What cannot move, and what is taxed

Three final things stand between the economy as described and the equilibrium.

What the forest holds

The supply curves say how much wood owners are willing to sell at each price. They say nothing of how much there is: a year's harvest cannot take more than the stands hold. That limit is not one per product, because the products share their stands. Softwood logs come from conifers, hardwood logs from broadleaves, and smallwood — thinnings and tops — from both, so a m³ of conifer can be sold as a log or as smallwood, but not as both.

Here is what each region can cut in a year, in Mm³, by kind of stand — about a fifth more than it cuts in a normal year:

stock = Dict("SEF" => (conifers =  11.0, broadleaves =   6.0),
             "SWF" => (conifers =  16.0, broadleaves =   4.0),
             "GEF" => (conifers =   7.0, broadleaves =  13.0),
             "OF"  => (conifers =   9.0, broadleaves =  18.0),
             "EU"  => (conifers = 520.0, broadleaves = 340.0),
             "NA"  => (conifers = 700.0, broadleaves = 190.0),
             "AS"  => (conifers = 360.0, broadleaves = 570.0),
             "RW"  => (conifers = 480.0, broadleaves = 610.0))
stands_of = Dict("swr" => [:conifers], "hwr" => [:broadleaves],
                 "smallwood" => [:conifers, :broadleaves])
primary = ["swr", "hwr", "smallwood"]

The rule that keeps every m³ counted once is the one of the Forest Sector Model FFSM: for every combination of the primary products, their total harvest cannot exceed the stock of all the stands at least one of them can come from. Softwood logs alone are bounded by the conifers; softwood logs and smallwood together by conifers and broadleaves, since smallwood can come from either. Checking each product on its own would not do — two products may each fit while together drawing on the same stands beyond what they hold — and checking every combination is exactly enough: by Hall's theorem, the harvests then fit into the stands without any m³ being counted twice.

With three products that is seven combinations per region. Each is a @limit: a linear constraint on the quantities of the model, here on the sum of the supplies of a combination. Inside @limit, supply(…) names the supply of the model, not the curves of the same name above:

combinations(xs) = [xs[[isodd(i >> (j - 1)) for j in eachindex(xs)]]
                    for i in 1:(2^length(xs) - 1)]
available(r, C, stock) = sum(getfield(stock[r], s) for s in union((stands_of[p] for p in C)...))

forest_limits(stock) =
    [@limit("$r: " * join(C, " + "),
            sum(supply(region = r, product = p) for p in C) <= available(r, C, stock))
     for r in regions for C in combinations(primary)]

And the rest of the world taxes its log exports, as several timber exporters do, to keep the wood at home for its own mills. Implementing scenarios

A scenario is the same economy with something changed: a curve shifted, a stock smaller, a price outside the model higher. The economy is plain data, so a scenario is a copy of it with some fields replaced, solved again, and read against the reference run. One small helper does the copying:

reconfigure(d; kwargs...) =
    MarketData(; (f => get(kwargs, f, getfield(d, f)) for f in fieldnames(MarketData))...)

Three scenarios follow: a storm that floods the Landes with salvaged pine, a dearer gas that sends households to wood fuel, and a third of the French forests set aside.

A storm in the Landes

In January 2009 the storm Klaus felled some 40 Mm³ of maritime pine in south-west France, several years of harvest at once. The salvage has to be sold, so the region's supply curve shifts far out.

That is what the shift field of a SupplyCurve is for. Everything about the curve other than its price response lives there — the standing volume, the road network, the labour and machines available — while p0 and q0 go on holding the calibration. Tripling q0 would have worked here too, but it would have destroyed the reference point the curve was fitted to; shifting leaves it intact, which is what lets a scenario be run, reported and reversed. For a driver with a known elasticity, exogenous_shift turns levels into the multiplier:

shift = exogenous_shift(growing_stock = (level = 118.0, reference = 100.0,
                                         elasticity = 0.6))

Here the salvage simply triples what the Landes offer at any price. The felled trees are also wood that can now be cut, so the storm adds them to the conifer stock of the Landes, as dead timber that can be salvaged — some 30 Mm³ of the 40 Klaus brought down. Everything else is left alone:

salvage = [s.region == "SWF" && s.product == "swr" ?
               SupplyCurve(s; shift = 3.0) : s
           for s in supply]

felled = merge(stock, Dict("SWF" => (conifers = stock["SWF"].conifers + 30.0,
                                     broadleaves = stock["SWF"].broadleaves)))
storm = solve_market(reconfigure(example_market, supply = salvage,
                                 limits = all_limits(felled)))

The gradient across the blocs is the Armington structure at work. That rent is what drives a wedge between SWF and the rest of the French market, and it is why a salvage price collapse is a local event: not because the wood is worth less in principle, but because it cannot reach anyone who would pay more for it.

Three of the features set up in this example meet in that one table — a bundle of substitutes in demand, a capacity bound on transformation, and a price from outside the model — and the result is not what any of them would have given alone.

Setting forests aside

The stands bind once there are fewer of them. Suppose a third of every French forest is set aside — protected, out of reach of the harvest — and nothing else changes:

protected = Dict(r => r in france ? (conifers = 2 / 3 * s.conifers, broadleaves = 2 / 3 * s.broadleaves) : s
                 for (r, s) in stock)
aside = solve_market(reconfigure(example_market, limits = all_limits(protected)))

A region whose wood had no close substitute would see it in its prices too.

The limits of uncalibrated data

Every curve in this model passes through a reference point taken from the data: each supply curve through a region's harvest at its roadside price, each demand curve through a region's consumption at the price given for the product. The harvests land within 2% of the data: logs trade freely, and the supply curves hold them close to their reference points. The prices do not. They miss the one price given by up to a fifth, and by different amounts in different regions — plywood costs 306 to 339 where 285 was given, softwood lumber 209 to 245 against 205.

The reason is that the numbers are not consistent with each other. One price per product says every region pays the same, while freight and the preference for local products say they cannot. The trade shares come from a rule, not from what was shipped, so the flows the model needs to feed each region are not those that made the observed harvests worth cutting. The mills' costs are guesses, so the margins they leave do not match the prices the mills paid and got. Each piece of data pulls the equilibrium its own way, and the equilibrium is wherever they balance — which is not the base year.

Does it matter? A scenario is read as a difference from the model's own reference run, not from the data, and for much of it the answer is no: constant-elasticity curves give the same percentage response wherever they are evaluated. But a difference also depends on what the shock runs into — how close a limit is, how large each market share is, where the mills run — and those the uncalibrated reference run gets wrong in its own way. How much that changes the storm and the gas scenarios is measured on the third page.

Getting the reference run onto the data is calibration: choosing the numbers nobody observes — trade shares, costs, the recipe of each mill, the reference point of each curve — so that the observed year is an equilibrium. The next page, Calibrating with partial data, does it with the incomplete and inconsistent statistics a real application has to work with.

The results of this page are kept, for that comparison:

results_dir = joinpath(pkgdir(Markets), "examples", "forest", "results")

function outcome(result, scenario)
    t = outerjoin(select(result.prices, :region, :product, :price, :producer_price),
                  rename(result.production, :quantity => :production),
                  select(result.consumption, :region, :product, :quantity => :consumption),
                  on = [:region, :product])
    return insertcols!(t, 1, :scenario => scenario)
end

function save_outcomes(file, runs)
    t = vcat((outcome(r, s) for (s, r) in runs)...)
    cell(x) = ismissing(x) ? "" : x isa Real ? string(round(x; sigdigits = 6)) : string(x)
    mkpath(results_dir)
    open(joinpath(results_dir, file), "w") do io
        println(io, join(names(t), ","))
        foreach(row -> println(io, join(cell.(collect(row)), ",")), eachrow(t))
    end
end

save_outcomes("uncalibrated.csv", ["reference" => res, "storm" => storm, "gas" => gas])

Where to go from here

  • Change the elasticities in σ_blocs and σ_europe and watch the gradient above widen or flatten, or those in σ_lumber, σ_construction and σ_fuel and watch demand move between products instead of between regions.
  • Raise or remove the pellet capacities and see how much of the energy shock moves from firewood to pellets.
  • Put a tariff on something and compare the objective with and without the revenue added back; the difference is the transfer the program counts as a cost.
  • Put real elasticities in. Buongiorno (2015) for the demand side, McDaniel & Balistreri (2003) for the Armington ones.
  • Add limits of your own with @limit: a minimum share of domestic logs in the French mills, a quota on exports, a cap on the smallwood that may be burnt — any linear constraint on the quantities of the model, with its rent reported in Results.limits.
  • Calibrate it: the next page replaces the gravity shares, the single prices and the guessed costs with numbers worked out from data.
  • Give the EU market its own destination specification to represent a non-tariff measure applying to one bloc only.
  • Make transport concave in distance, or compute distances between the centres of gravity of the forest resource and of the population rather than between region centroids.
  • Drive a multi-period run from outside: call solve_market once per period and update each region's supply shift from the standing volume left after the harvest, and each mill's capacity limit from what was built. The engine stays static on purpose — the time loop belongs to whatever uses it.
