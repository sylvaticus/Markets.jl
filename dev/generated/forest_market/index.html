The forest sector: France in the world

This example builds a complete spatial partial-equilibrium model of the forest-products sector with Markets.jl the way a real application would: starting from observed base-year data, adding the behavioural parameters one year of data cannot tell, deriving everything else by calibration, and then checking how close the model lands to the data it was built from before asking it anything new.

For illustrative purposes only

The data are plausible, but they are not statistics. They are rounded the way published figures are, small flows are missing as they are from trade statistics, and they do not balance exactly — like real data, and unlike an equilibrium. The model therefore lands near them rather than on them, and the page shows by how much.

The structure of the model lives in this script and the data in the CSV files of examples/forest/data, as they should in any application: the same script runs on another base year by pointing it at other files.

The economy has eight regions. France is split into four regions and the rest of the world into four blocs:

SEFSouth-east France — the Alps and the Massif Central, spruce and fir
SWFSouth-west France — the Landes, Europe's largest maritime-pine plantation
GEFGrand Est — the Vosges, and the oak and beech of the north-east
OFthe rest of France
EUthe rest of the European Union
NANorth America
ASAsia-Pacific
RWthe rest of the world

What makes the regional detail work is the way the varieties of a product substitute for each other, the subject of the Armington section below: French products are near-perfect substitutes for each other, close substitutes for the rest of the EU, and more distant substitutes for the other blocs.

The sector modelled here is the one the Global Forest Products Model established as the reference, Buongiorno et al. (2003); for the same economics in working open-source code, with different choices about dynamics and data, see TiMBA, [C3].

Quantities are in million m³ for wood products and million tonnes for pulp, paper and pellets; prices are in USD per m³ or per tonne.

using Markets, DataFrames, DelimitedFiles

Regions and products

france  = ["SEF", "SWF", "GEF", "OF"]
regions = [france..., "EU", "NA", "AS", "RW"]

The product list follows the FAOSTAT / UNECE chain, splitting roundwood by coniferous (softwood, swr) and non-coniferous (hardwood, hwr), because the two lead to different products.

smallwood is the third assortment the forest yields: small-diameter roundwood from thinnings and tops, too poor for sawing. In French statistics it is the bois d'industrie et bois énergie category, and the name says what matters here — the same log can be burnt in a stove or sent to a mill, and which it is depends on what the two uses are willing to pay.

products = ["swr", "hwr", "smallwood", "chips", "pulp",
            "sawn_sw", "sawn_hw", "panel", "plywood", "paper", "pellets"]

They are connected by the mills set up further down:

  FOREST ── swr ───────┬─ sawmill_sw ─→ sawn_sw + chips
         ── hwr ───────┼─ sawmill_hw ─→ sawn_hw + chips
         ── smallwood ─┤
                       ├─ plymill    : CES(swr, hwr) ───────────────────→ plywood + chips
                       ├─ panelmill  : CES(chips, smallwood, swr, hwr) ─→ panel
                       ├─ pulpmill   : CES(swr, hwr, chips, smallwood) ─→ pulp ─→ papermill ─→ paper
                       └─ pelletmill : CES(chips, smallwood, swr, hwr) ─→ pellets

                          smallwood ─────────────────────────────────→ burnt as firewood

Two things in that picture are worth more than a passing glance.

chips are a sawmill and plymill by-product and an input to three other mills, so the competition for residues is part of the equilibrium rather than an assumption about it.

smallwood is both a final product and an intermediate one. It comes out of the forest, and from there it either goes into a household stove as firewood — final demand, like sawnwood or paper — or into a mill as a raw material. The model does not need to be told which: both uses face the same local price, and the split between them is whatever that price makes optimal. A product in this engine may be primary-supplied, manufactured, consumed and used as an input all at once; its balance simply sums the sources and the uses.

Three kinds of numbers

Everything the model needs falls into one of three groups, and keeping them apart is most of the discipline of building one.

  1. Observed: what happened in the base year — what each region produced, consumed and shipped, at what prices, with what plants. It comes from statistics, and is read from files below.
  2. Chosen: how the economy responds, which a single year cannot show — elasticities of supply and demand, and how easily one product or one origin replaces another. They come from the literature or from your own estimation, and are written in this script, where they can be argued about.
  3. Calibrated: everything else — the reference points of the curves, the shares of every CES, the costs of the mills. These are never guessed: they are whatever makes the observed year an equilibrium of the chosen behaviour.

The model is then a machine for answering what if: it reproduces the base year, which you knew, and moves away from it consistently, which you did not.

The base year, as observed

The data are long-format CSV files, one observation per row, which is the shape statistics come in. DelimitedFiles, from the standard library, is enough to read them; in a real application you would more likely reach for CSV.jl.

data = pkgdir(Markets, "examples", "forest", "data")

function read_table(name)
    cells, header = readdlm(joinpath(data, name * ".csv"), ',', header = true)
    text(c) = c isa AbstractString ? String(c) : c
    return DataFrame([String(h) => identity.(text.(cells[:, j]))
                      for (j, h) in enumerate(vec(header))])
end

    Dict(Tuple(row[k] for k in keys) => row[value] for row in eachrow(table))

What it costs to carry

Freight is charged per unit of quantity, not of value, because shipping a m³ of logs costs much the same whether the logs are cheap or dear. Bulky, low-value products therefore carry a high transport cost relative to their price, which is what keeps roundwood trade regional and lets paper travel. Distances are relative, with the Europe–North America crossing as the unit.

freight   = read_table("freight")
distances = read_table("distance")

dist = lookup(distances, :distance, :from, :to)
distance(a, b) = get(dist, (a, b), get(dist, (b, a), 0.0))
rate = Dict(zip(freight.product, freight.cost))
transport_costs = Dict((p, a, b) => rate[p] * distance(a, b)
                       for p in products, a in regions, b in regions if a != b)

The conversion factors are engineering, and the same everywhere: a m³ of sawlog yields half a m³ of lumber and a third of a m³ of chips, a tonne of paper takes 1.1 tonnes of pulp. They are written into the processes below.

Policies and logistics

The rest of the world taxes its log exports, as several timber exporters do, to keep the wood at home for its own mills. The rate is ad valorem, a quarter of the price at the border, which is the one kind of wedge this model cannot write into its objective — the price is a dual of the problem. It is found by iteration instead, which solve_market does on its own; see Why ad valorem rates on trade are special.

log_duty = 0.25
taxes = [Tax(name = "rw_log_duty", on = "trade", product = ["swr", "hwr"], from = "RW",
             rate = log_duty, relative = true)]
duty(p, from) = from == "RW" && p in ("swr", "hwr") ? log_duty : 0.0

Wood leaving the Landes has to be carried on the roads and through the ports that are there. A TradeLimit caps the sum of the flows it covers, so one entry limits all the roundwood SWF can send anywhere. At 18 Mm³ it is slack in the base year, and the storm scenario at the end is what makes it bite.

trade_limits = [TradeLimit(name = "landes_evacuation", products = ["swr", "smallwood"],
                           from = "SWF", capacity = 18.0)]

Finally the price of gas, which wood fuel competes with but this sector does not determine. Only its base-year level matters here: it is the reference the demand for wood fuel is calibrated at.

gas_reference = 30.0

What one year cannot tell

A single observation of each market is a point. The curves through those points need slopes, and the slopes come from elsewhere.

Supply. How much more wood the forest gives when it pays more. French forests are fragmented between many small private owners, which makes the harvest slow to respond to price, so their elasticities are lower than the blocs'.

supply_elasticity = Dict(
    "swr"       => Dict("SEF" => 0.40, "SWF" => 0.45, "GEF" => 0.40, "OF" => 0.40,
                        "EU"  => 0.60, "NA"  => 0.60, "AS"  => 0.55, "RW" => 0.55),
    "hwr"       => Dict("SEF" => 0.35, "SWF" => 0.35, "GEF" => 0.35, "OF" => 0.35,
                        "EU"  => 0.50, "NA"  => 0.50, "AS"  => 0.50, "RW" => 0.50),
    "smallwood" => Dict("SEF" => 0.50, "SWF" => 0.55, "GEF" => 0.50, "OF" => 0.50,
                        "EU"  => 0.60, "NA"  => 0.60, "AS"  => 0.55, "RW" => 0.55))

Demand. The own-price elasticities are below 1: demand for forest products is usually found to be price-inelastic, construction lumber and paper most of all, since their cost is a small part of the building or the printed product they end up in. An inelastic demand makes prices, rather than quantities, absorb a supply shock — which matters for the storm at the end.

They are not given product by product, because the products are not separate markets. Softwood lumber and panels do the same job in a building and a builder swaps one for the other readily; hardwood lumber is a more distant substitute for either. Pellets and firewood are the same heat in different packaging. Paper competes with nothing else here. Each group gets one curve, with its own elasticity, and an elasticity of substitution σ saying how its members trade off against each other.

η_construction, η_fuel, η_paper = 0.45, 0.35, 0.30
σ_lumber, σ_construction, σ_fuel = 5.0, 1.6, 3.0
lumber = ["sawn_sw", "panel", "plywood"]
fuels  = ["smallwood", "pellets"]

Wood fuel also competes with gas, and the cross-price elasticity says how much: a dearer gas shifts the whole wood-fuel curve out.

gas_cross_elasticity = 0.35

Mills. How readily each mill switches between the kinds of wood it can use when one of them gets dearer.

σ_mills = Dict("plymill" => 1.8, "panelmill" => 2.5, "pulpmill" => 1.8, "pelletmill" => 3.0)

Who substitutes for whom

Last, and most consequential: how the varieties of a product from different origins replace each other. If they were perfect substitutes everywhere, each market would simply buy from the cheapest source and a small cost advantage would take everything. If instead they were all imperfect substitutes with one elasticity, French oak would be no closer to French pine than to Asian hardwood, which is just as wrong.

What the data say is a hierarchy:

  • inside France the product of one region is the same good as that of another — a m³ of Landes pine and a m³ of Vosges pine of the same grade are interchangeable, and only transport separates them;
  • between France and the rest of the EU the products are close but not identical: shared standards and certification, short distances, but different species mixes and supply contracts;
  • between the big blocs they are more distant substitutes: different species, grading rules, phytosanitary requirements and commercial habits.

That hierarchy is a tree, which is exactly what nested OriginNests express: the French regions together at a very high elasticity, then France and the rest of the EU at a high one, then Europe as a whole against the other blocs at a lower one.

σ_blocs = Dict("swr" => 6.0, "hwr" => 6.0, "smallwood" => 7.0, "chips" => 7.0,
               "pulp" => 5.0, "sawn_sw" => 3.5, "sawn_hw" => 3.0, "panel" => 3.0,
               "plywood" => 2.5, "paper" => 4.0, "pellets" => 6.0)
σ_europe = Dict(p => 3σ for (p, σ) in σ_blocs)
σ_france = 30.0

europe(p) = OriginNest(sigma   = σ_europe[p],
                       origins = ["EU"],
                       nests   = [OriginNest(sigma = σ_france, origins = france)])

Commodities graded to a standard (chips, roundwood, pulp) substitute readily between blocs; manufactured products, where origin carries a reputation, do so less. Within Europe every product substitutes three times more easily than between blocs, and within France more easily still. Note the direction of the inequality the package enforces: a group must hold together at least as tightly as it holds to the outside, 30 ≥ 3σ ≥ σ. The reverse would say that members of a group are worse substitutes for each other than for outsiders, which no cost function can represent.

Why not make the French regions perfect substitutes, sigma = Inf, and France a single market up to transport? Because with mills that have constant returns, that would leave the model unable to say where in France the milling happens — see Perfect substitutes and constant returns for why, and for the remedies. A finite elasticity, however high, gives each region's product a demand of its own, which settles it.

From data to model

Now the third kind of number: every parameter that is neither observed nor chosen, worked out so that the base year is an equilibrium.

made     = lookup(production, :quantity, :region, :product)
πˢ       = lookup(producer_prices, :price, :region, :product)   # producer prices
consumed = lookup(consumption, :quantity, :region, :product)

Every flow, including the one nobody reports

What a region keeps of its own variety is what it made less what it exported. With it the flows form a full origin × destination matrix per product, the diagonal included:

flows = lookup(trade, :quantity, :product, :from, :to)
exported(r, p) = sum(x for ((q, o, _), x) in flows if q == p && o == r; init = 0.0)
for ((r, p), q) in made
    flows[(p, r, r)] = q - exported(r, p)
end
minimum(values(flows))
0.00125

Not negative anywhere, which is the first check a base year has to pass: a region cannot export more than it makes.

The price at the destination

A variety costs its buyer what its producer was paid, plus the duty if there is one, plus the freight to get it there:

delivered(p, o, r) = o == r ? πˢ[(o, p)] :
                     πˢ[(o, p)] * (1 + duty(p, o)) + transport_costs[(p, o, r)]

The second price: what users pay

Each region buys a mix of varieties, its own and imported ones, at different delivered prices, so two prices coexist in every region: what its producers get for their variety, read from the statistics above, and what its users pay for the mix. The second is not a separate observation. It is the average delivered price of what the region bought,

\[\pi^c_r = \frac{\sum_o X_{o,r}\, q_{o,r}}{\sum_o X_{o,r}},\]

the value of its purchases divided by their volume — which is how a statistical office computes a consumer unit value, and also the price the calibrated model charges for the mix when it reproduces the base year.

πᶜ = Dict{Tuple{String,String},Float64}()
for r in regions, p in products
    bought = [(o, x) for ((q, o, d), x) in flows if q == p && d == r && x > 0]
    isempty(bought) && continue
    πᶜ[(r, p)] = sum(x * delivered(p, o, r) for (o, x) in bought) / sum(last, bought)
end

Trade shares

A destination's composite is a CES over its origins, and its shares $\delta$ are not the observed shares: they are the shares each origin would have if all delivered prices were equal, so they carry the home bias and the trade pattern that prices alone do not explain. Inverting the CES demand condition gives them from the flows and the delivered prices, level by level of the tree; armington_calibration does it for a whole product at once.

It also sets one more number per destination, the scale of the composite. A CES of quantities equals their plain sum only where their prices are equal; elsewhere it falls short, by a few per cent for the French roundwood here. The scale restores the sum at the base year, so that the composite is counted in m³ and priced at $\pi^c_r$, and the m³ the flows bring in are the m³ the mills and the households take out.

armington = [armington_calibration(
                 product = p, sigma = σ_blocs[p], nests = [europe(p)],
                 flows   = Dict((o, r) => x for ((q, o, r), x) in flows if q == p),
                 prices  = Dict((o, r) => delivered(p, o, r)
                                for o in regions, r in regions if haskey(πˢ, (o, p))))
             for p in products]

A route that carried nothing in the base year — or too little to be reported — gets no share, and stays closed whatever prices do: the well-known limit of Armington calibration.

Supply

Each forest supply curve runs through the observed harvest at the observed producer price, with the chosen slope:

supply = [SupplySpec(product = p, region = r, p0 = πˢ[(r, p)], q0 = made[(r, p)],
                     elasticity = supply_elasticity[p][r])
          for p in keys(supply_elasticity) for r in regions]

Final demand

A curve over a bundle of products is a DemandNest: a tree of products with one demand curve on the bundle. It has to be a tree rather than a matrix of cross-price elasticities, for the reason set out in Substitution in final demand — a free matrix is not the gradient of any benefit function, and the model would stop being the market it claims to represent.

Calibrating a bundle

A demand curve over a bundle needs three numbers that no statistic reports: the shares of its members, and the price and quantity of the bundle itself, which are the p0 and q0 of its curve. What the data give instead is a consumer price and a quantity for each product. ces_calibration turns the second into the first, choosing the three so that at the base-year prices the model buys exactly the base-year quantities. For the wood fuel of OF:

ces_calibration(quantities = [consumed[("OF", p)] for p in fuels],
                prices     = [πᶜ[("OF", p)] for p in fuels], sigma = σ_fuel)
(shares = [0.036631572034021155, 0.9633684279659788], price = 222.59653134657145, quantity = 3.7200646639020993, scale = 1.8225489642129586)
  • The shares are what each product's share would be if both cost the same. Pellets get 96% of the bundle although households in OF buy three times more firewood: pellets are bought little because they are dearer, and would be bought far more at an equal price.
  • The price is the CES price index of the bundle — the cost of one unit of it at the base-year prices.
  • The quantity is the base-year spending divided by that price. It is an index number, not a volume: m³ of firewood and tonnes of pellets would mean nothing added up, while their values at a common price do. That is what a CES composite is.

The prices are consumer prices, $\pi^c$: the household in OF buys the mix of French and imported pellets, not the pellets of OF.

Wood in buildings

Construction is a nest inside a nest — softwood lumber, particleboard and plywood together at σ_lumber, hardwood lumber against that group at σ_construction. The three in the inner group are what a builder actually chooses between for a floor or a wall; appearance-grade hardwood is a different kind of purchase. A nested bundle is calibrated from the inside out: the inner group on its three products; its price and quantity then make it one member of the outer bundle, next to hardwood lumber; and the outer price and quantity are the reference point of the curve.

function construction_demand(r)
    inner = ces_calibration(quantities = [consumed[(r, p)] for p in lumber],
                            prices     = [πᶜ[(r, p)] for p in lumber], sigma = σ_lumber)
    outer = ces_calibration(quantities = [consumed[(r, "sawn_hw")], inner.quantity],
                            prices     = [πᶜ[(r, "sawn_hw")], inner.price],
                            sigma = σ_construction)
    return DemandSpec(region = r, p0 = outer.price, q0 = outer.quantity,
        elasticity = η_construction,
        product = DemandNest(sigma = σ_construction,
            products = ["sawn_hw"], shares = [outer.shares[1]],
            nests = [DemandNest(sigma = σ_lumber, share = outer.shares[2],
                                products = lumber, shares = inner.shares)]))
end

η_construction is the own-price elasticity of the whole bundle: how much less wood goes into buildings when all of it gets dearer. How the bundle splits when only one product gets dearer is the job of the two σ.

Wood for heat

Wood fuel is a single nest, and the place where the price of gas comes in. An exogenous price needs no nest at all — being a constant, it shifts the whole curve — so the cross-price elasticity goes into exogenous_shift. At the reference level the shifter is exactly 1, and the curve is the calibrated one; the scenario at the end moves it.

function fuel_demand(r, gas = gas_reference)
    fuel = ces_calibration(quantities = [consumed[(r, p)] for p in fuels],
                           prices     = [πᶜ[(r, p)] for p in fuels], sigma = σ_fuel)
    return DemandSpec(region = r, p0 = fuel.price, q0 = fuel.quantity, elasticity = η_fuel,
        shift = exogenous_shift(gas = (level = gas, reference = gas_reference,
                                       elasticity = gas_cross_elasticity)),
        product = DemandNest(sigma = σ_fuel, products = fuels, shares = fuel.shares))
end

Paper competes with nothing here, so it keeps a plain curve through its own consumer price and consumption. The whole demand side is then one curve per bundle and region, built by a function of the gas price so that the gas scenario at the end can rebuild it at another level:

The mills

Seven processes, of three kinds.

Fixed proportions with a by-product. Sawmilling turns a log into lumber and chips in set amounts. The plywood mill is the same shape — veneer is peeled from the log, and what is left, nearly half of it, comes out as chips — except that its input is a CES bundle of the two roundwood grades, because a veneer mill can work softwood or hardwood and will lean toward whichever is cheaper.

A CES bundle of wood. Panel, pulp and pellet mills draw on a mix of chips, smallwood and roundwood, so when chips become scarce — because the other mills are bidding for them — each shifts smoothly toward roundwood.

Fixed proportions, single input. The paper mill takes pulp and nothing else.

A bundle is calibrated like a demand nest, from the observed mix at the consumer prices of the region it was observed in. Its quantities are all m³ of wood, so here they do add up, and the scale of the calibration lets the bundle keep counting m³ — the composite is then the m³ of wood a unit of output takes, as the conversion factor says it:

function bundle(process)
    mix = input_mix[input_mix.process .== process, :]
    c = ces_calibration(quantities = mix.quantity,
                        prices = [πᶜ[(r, p)] for (r, p) in zip(mix.region, mix.product)],
                        sigma = σ_mills[process])
    return ces(composite = sum(mix.quantity), products = mix.product, shares = c.shares,
               sigma = σ_mills[process], phi = c.scale)
end

What the mills cost

The vacost of a process is the cost of everything not modelled as an input: labour, energy, capital. No statistic gives it in the units the model wants, but the base year implies it. A mill that ran and did not expand earned no more than its costs, so its cost is its margin: what its outputs fetched at the producer prices of its region, less what its inputs cost at the consumer prices there.

For a CES bundle the cost of the inputs is the price index of the bundle at the region's prices — which is what the cheapest mix costs there — per m³ of the bundle, hence the division by its scale:

price_index(δ, p, σ) = sum(δ .* p .^ (1 - σ))^(1 / (1 - σ))
input_cost(n::Nest, r) = length(n.products) == 1 ?
    n.composite * πᶜ[(r, only(n.products))] :
    n.composite * price_index(n.shares, [πᶜ[(r, p)] for p in n.products], n.sigma) / n.phi
margin(inputs, outputs, r) = sum(y * πˢ[(r, k)] for (k, y) in outputs) -
                             sum(input_cost(n, r) for n in inputs)

The margins differ from region to region, as wages and energy do, which is why a process takes a region => cost dictionary. They are positive everywhere, the second check a base year has to pass: a negative one would say a mill ran at a loss, and the model would close it.

The margin is taken as all cost. A mill that ran flat out could have earned a rent on top, and one year cannot tell the two apart; that is why the capacities in the data are those of plants with room to spare.

How close does the model land to the data?

Every curve, share and cost above was fitted to one observation, and each passes through it exactly: the supply curve of SWF through its harvest at its producer price, the demand for paper in OF through its consumption at its consumer price, the trade shares of each destination through its flows. But the observations come from different sources, rounded, with small flows missing, and they are not quite consistent with each other — the wood a region took in does not add up to exactly what its households and its mills used. An equilibrium has to satisfy every balance at once, so it settles near every observation and on none.

How near is the first thing to look at, every time — benchmark replication:

A storm in the Landes

In January 2009 the storm Klaus felled some 40 Mm³ of maritime pine in south-west France, several years of harvest at once. The salvage has to be sold, so the region's supply curve shifts far out.

That is what the shift field of a SupplySpec is for. Everything about the curve other than its price response lives there — the standing volume, the road network, the labour and machines available — while p0 and q0 go on holding the calibration. Tripling q0 would have worked here too, but it would have destroyed the reference point the curve was fitted to; shifting leaves it intact, which is what lets a scenario be run, reported and reversed. For a driver with a known elasticity, exogenous_shift turns levels into the multiplier:

shift = exogenous_shift(growing_stock = (level = 118.0, reference = 100.0,
                                         elasticity = 0.6))

Here the salvage simply triples what the Landes offer at any price, and everything else is left alone:

Two things are going on at once, and they are worth separating.

The gradient across the blocs is the Armington structure at work. They could grow only into the room they had spare, so pellet consumption grows least in the rest of the world, whose plants were the fullest, and most in North America, whose plants had the most room. The rest of the extra demand lands on the fuel that needs no plant: a log in a stove.

Three of the features set up in this example meet in that one table — a bundle of substitutes in demand, a capacity bound on transformation, and a price from outside the model — and the result is not what any of them would have given alone.

Where to go from here

  • Point data at your own base year. The script does not care where the numbers came from, only that they balance; the replication table says whether they do.
  • Change the elasticities in σ_blocs and σ_europe and watch the storm's gradient widen or flatten, or those in σ_lumber, σ_construction and σ_fuel and watch demand move between products instead of between regions. None of them changes the base year — that is what calibration guarantees — only how the economy moves away from it.
  • Raise σ_france and watch France draw together into one market, or lower it and watch the regions drift apart; remove the pellet capacities and see how much of the energy shock moves from firewood to pellets.
  • Put real elasticities in. Buongiorno (2015) for the demand side, McDaniel & Balistreri (2003) for the Armington ones.
  • Give the EU market its own destination specification to represent a non-tariff measure applying to one bloc only.
  • Drive a multi-period run from outside: call solve_market once per period and update each region's supply shift from the standing volume left after the harvest, and each mill's capacity from what was built. The engine stays static on purpose — the time loop belongs to whatever uses it, and the calibration above stays where it is for the whole run.
