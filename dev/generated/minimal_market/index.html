Two regions and a dairy

The smallest model that is still a market: two regions, two products, one transformation and one trade route. Everything else in this package is more of these same four things.

The Pasture has the cows; the City has the people. Milk is either drunk as it is or turned into cheese, in either place, and either milk or cheese can be carried between the two at a cost. Four questions follow, and none of them has an obvious answer: how much of the milk is drunk and how much goes to the dairy, where the dairy stands, what each region pays, and what travels. Nobody decides. The answer is whatever clears both markets at once.

using Markets, DataFrames

The economy

Two regions and two products. Quantities are tonnes, prices euros per tonne.

regions  = ["Pasture", "City"]
products = ["milk", "cheese"]

Milk comes from the herds, on a curve through one observed point: last year the Pasture sold 2000 at €400 the tonne. The City keeps a few herds at its edges, where land is dear.

supply = [SupplyCurve(product = "milk", region = "Pasture", p0 = 400, q0 = 2000, elasticity = 0.6),
          SupplyCurve(product = "milk", region = "City",    p0 = 450, q0 =  300, elasticity = 0.6)]

Now the part that makes this more than an arithmetic exercise: milk is both a final product and an input. People drink it, and dairies turn it into cheese. Each use has its own demand, and neither is told how much milk it may have.

demand = [DemandCurve(product = "milk",   region = "Pasture", p0 =  400, q0 = 200, elasticity = 0.3),
          DemandCurve(product = "milk",   region = "City",    p0 =  450, q0 = 600, elasticity = 0.3),
          DemandCurve(product = "cheese", region = "Pasture", p0 = 5300, q0 =  30, elasticity = 0.5),
          DemandCurve(product = "cheese", region = "City",    p0 = 5350, q0 = 120, elasticity = 0.5)]

One transformation, and it may run in either region. Cheesemaking is a fixed recipe — ten tonnes of milk to the tonne of cheese, the rest leaving as whey and water — and €1300 covers everything the model does not name: the rennet, the cellar, the cheesemaker.

processes = [Process(name    = "dairy",
                     inputs  = [leontief(product = "milk", coeff = 10.0)],
                     outputs = ["cheese" => 1.0],
                     vacost  = 1300)]

Both products can be carried either way, at €45 the tonne. Writing a route down is what makes a product tradable — anything with no route stays where it was made — and routes are directional, hence four entries for two products. Note that the rate is the same for both: nothing in the data says that one is cheaper to move than the other.

transport_costs = Dict(("milk",   "Pasture", "City") => 45.0, ("milk",   "City", "Pasture") => 45.0,
                       ("cheese", "Pasture", "City") => 45.0, ("cheese", "City", "Pasture") => 45.0)

Looking before solving

The pieces go into one MarketData. Typing its name gives back only how much it holds; printing it gives a summary of the economy that was actually written down: what each product does, how the processes chain, where goods may go and at what cost. It is the cheapest check there is that the data say what was meant.

Check the signs against `print`

JuMP 1.32 drops the minus sign of a negative coefficient written in scientific notation when it typesets a model (JuMP.jl#4268). The two milk terms at the start of the objective above are such coefficients: both are negative, as print(jump_model(economy)) shows. Until the bug is fixed, read the signs from print.

Reading the program

There are four kinds of variable, and each is named after where it lives:

  • D[region,product] is final demand, the quantity consumed: D[City,milk] is the milk the City drinks.
  • S[region,product] is primary supply: S[Pasture,milk] is the milk the Pasture's herds give.
  • z[region,process] is the activity level of a process in a region: z[Pasture,dairy] is how much the Pasture's dairy works, and z[City,dairy] how much the City's does. The first index is the location and the second names the process; neither is an input or an output. What the dairy takes and gives is in its recipe: each unit of activity takes 10 tonnes of milk and yields 1 tonne of cheese, so here one unit of activity is one tonne of cheese.
  • T[product,from,to] is the quantity shipped on a route: T[milk,Pasture,City] is the milk carried from the Pasture to the City. There is one for each route declared in transport_costs, so four here.

Constraints

The constraints are of two kinds. The first is a material balance, balance[region,product], for every product in every region: what is supplied, made and imported equals what is consumed, used up and exported.

\[S_{r,p} + \sum_k y_{k,p}\, z_{r,k} + \sum_o T_{p,o,r} \;=\; D_{r,p} + \sum_k u_{k,p}\, z_{r,k} + \sum_d T_{p,r,d}\]

$k$ runs over the processes, and this economy has only one, dairy. For each process $k$ and product $p$:

  • $y_{k,p}$ is how much of $p$ one unit of activity of $k$ produces: its yield, from outputs;
  • $u_{k,p}$ is how much of $p$ one unit of activity of $k$ uses up: its input requirement, from inputs.

For the dairy they are

product$y_{\text{dairy},p}$ (out)$u_{\text{dairy},p}$ (in)
milk010
cheese10

so in region $r$, $\sum_k y_{k,p}\, z_{r,k}$ is everything the processes there make of $p$, and $\sum_k u_{k,p}\, z_{r,k}$ everything they use up of it. With one process each sum has a single term that is not zero, and the two balances of a region read

milk:    S + 0·z + imports = D + 10·z + exports
cheese:  0 + 1·z + imports = D +  0·z + exports

which is where the coefficients of z in the printed model come from: −10 in the milk balance of its region, +1 in the cheese balance.

What is transformed is in neither S nor D. S is primary supply only, what enters from outside the modelled processes: the milk of the herds. D is final demand only, what leaves the economy to be consumed: the milk people drink and the cheese they eat. The milk the dairy takes is the $u\, z$ term, and the cheese it makes the $y\, z$ one.

That the balance is this simple comes from the dairy's recipe being fixed — a leontief input, ten tonnes of milk whatever the prices. Each $u_{k,p}$ is then a constant, and what a process uses is that constant times its activity. A process whose inputs substitute for each other chooses its mix as prices move, and the quantity it uses of each becomes a variable of its own; the forest example has those.

The dual value of each balance is the price of that product in that region.

The second kind of constraint is the bounds. S, z and T are only required to be non-negative. Each D is instead kept a little above zero, by a floor of a millionth of its own reference quantity — its q0, times its shift, which is 1 here:

variableq0floor
D[Pasture,milk]2000.0002
D[City,milk]6000.0006
D[Pasture,cheese]300.00003
D[City,cheese]1200.00012

The printed model shows them as 0.00019999999999999998 and the like, which is only floating point: a millionth of 200 has no exact binary form.

The floor is there because, with $\eta < 1$, the demand term of the objective below raises D to a negative power — $D^{-2.33}$ for milk, $D^{-1}$ for cheese — which is infinite at zero, and so is its slope. The solver evaluates both at every step it takes, so D must never get there, not even on the way to the answer. At a solution the floor never binds: as consumption shrinks towards zero its price grows without limit, and nobody buys so little. S needs no floor, because it is raised to a power above 1 ($S^{2.67}$), smooth at zero; nor do z and T, which enter only linearly.

The floor is per variable because what counts as close to zero depends on the quantity: a millionth of its own reference is negligible for each, whether a product trades in tens of tonnes or in millions. A single absolute floor would have to suit the smallest product, and would then be far smaller than needed for the largest. The qfloor keyword of jump_model and solve_market replaces them with one value for every variable.

The objective

What is maximised is the net social surplus: what consumers would pay for what they get, less what it costs to produce, make and carry. Written with its parameters rather than its numbers, it is

\[\max_{D,\,S,\,z,\,T}\; \sum_{r,p} \frac{a_{r,p}}{1 - 1/\eta_{r,p}}\, D_{r,p}^{\,1 - 1/\eta_{r,p}} \;-\; \sum_{r,p} \frac{b_{r,p}}{1 + 1/\varepsilon_{r,p}}\, S_{r,p}^{\,1 + 1/\varepsilon_{r,p}} \;-\; \sum_{r,k} c_k\, z_{r,k} \;-\; \sum_{p,o,d} \tau_{p,o,d}\, T_{p,o,d}\]

Each of the four sums is one block of the objective printed above.

  • Gross consumer benefit. Each demand curve, with reference point $(q_0, p_0)$, elasticity $\eta$ and shifter $m$, has the inverse $P^D(D) = a\, D^{-1/\eta}$, and the term is its antiderivative. Its slope at any $D$ is the price consumers would pay for one more unit.
  • Cost of primary supply. Each supply curve, with elasticity $\varepsilon$, has the inverse $P^S(S) = b\, S^{1/\varepsilon}$, and the term is the area under it: what the herds cost to keep, unit by unit.
  • Cost of making, $c_k$ per unit of activity: the vacost of €1300 for each tonne the dairy makes, in either region.
  • Cost of carrying, $\tau$ per unit shipped: the €45 of each route.

The scales $a$ and $b$ are what put each curve through its reference point. The curve must give the price $p_0$ at the quantity $m q_0$, the reference quantity as shifted, so

\[a \left(m q_0\right)^{-1/\eta} = p_0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; a = p_0 \left(m q_0\right)^{1/\eta}, \qquad b \left(m q_0\right)^{1/\varepsilon} = p_0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; b = p_0 \left(m q_0\right)^{-1/\varepsilon}\]

and the coefficient the printed objective shows in front of each power is that scale divided by the exponent. For milk in the Pasture, $p_0 = 400$, $q_0 = 200$, $m = 1$ and $\eta = 0.3$ give $a = 400 \times 200^{1/0.3} = 1.871 \times 10^{10}$ and the exponent $1 - 1/0.3 = -2.33$, so the coefficient is $1.871 \times 10^{10} / (-2.33) = -8.02 \times 10^9$. Every curve of the economy, worked out the same way:

The demand exponent and coefficient are both negative whenever $\eta < 1$, and the term still rises with $D$; for cheese, $\eta = 0.5$ makes the exponent exactly −1. The sizes of the scales say nothing in themselves: $a$ is a price times a quantity raised to $1/\eta$, and is large because $1/\eta$ is.

Why maximising this gives the market is in the conditions for its optimum. With $\pi_{r,p}$ the dual of each balance, they say that consumers buy until the price they would pay for one more unit, $P^D(D)$, falls to $\pi_{r,p}$. Herds produce until their marginal cost $P^S(S)$ rises to it. A dairy runs only where $\pi_{r,\text{cheese}} = 10\, \pi_{r,\text{milk}} + 1300$. A route is used only where the price gap across it is exactly $\tau$. Those are the conditions of a competitive market, and the last two are checked by hand further down. The general program, with taxes, bounds and imperfect substitutes, is on the Code implementation page.

Solving

result  = solve_market(economy)

Three things in those tables were never put in.

The milk splits itself. Of the 2301 tonnes the herds give, 801 are drunk and 1500 go to the dairy. Nothing in the data said how to divide them: the two uses bid against each other for the same milk, and the price that settles the contest is the one that makes every market balance at once.

The City makes no cheese at all — its entry in the production table is missing, meaning the dairy never runs there. Bringing in a tonne of cheese costs €45 of freight; bringing in the ten tonnes of milk behind it costs €450. So every cheese eaten in the City was made in the Pasture, and the model was not told that, it worked it out.

Milk still crosses, but only the milk that is drunk. The City's own herds cover part of its thirst and the rest is carried in. Not one tonne of milk is carried in order to be made into cheese.

Two things worth checking by hand

A model is worth no more than the conditions it satisfies, and in one this small each can be checked on a single line.

The dairy earns no profit. Where it runs, the cheese price must cover exactly ten tonnes of milk plus the €1300 — any more and another dairy would open, any less and this one would shut:

price(r, p) = only(result.prices[(result.prices.region .== r) .&
                                 (result.prices.product .== p), :price])
(cheese = price("Pasture", "cheese"), milk_plus_making = 10 * price("Pasture", "milk") + 1300)
(cheese = 5308.545270473618, milk_plus_making = 5308.545270474511)

Carrying earns no profit either. On a route that is used, the price gap between its two ends is exactly the freight — any wider and somebody would ship more until it closed. Both routes carry something here, so both gaps are €45:

(milk   = price("City", "milk")   - price("Pasture", "milk"),
 cheese = price("City", "cheese") - price("Pasture", "cheese"))
(milk = 44.99999999955929, cheese = 44.99999999888405)

Asking it something

The tables above are not the point — you could have measured those. The point is the question you cannot measure: what if something changed?

Suppose the City takes to drinking milk, and wants a fifth more of it at every price. That is a shifter on the demand curve; the observed point the curve was fitted to stays exactly where it was.

thirsty = [d.product == "milk" && d.region == "City" ?
               DemandCurve(d; shift = 1.2) : d
           for d in demand]
thirst = solve_market(MarketData(; regions, products, demand = thirsty, supply,
                                   processes, transport_costs))

after(r, p) = only(thirst.prices[(thirst.prices.region .== r) .&
                                 (thirst.prices.product .== p), :price])
DataFrame((region = r, product = p, before = round(price(r, p); digits = 1),
           after  = round(after(r, p); digits = 1),
           change_pct = round(100 * (after(r, p) / price(r, p) - 1); digits = 1))
          for r in regions for p in products)

Nobody's taste for cheese changed, and yet cheese is dearer — in the Pasture as much as in the City. The two uses of milk compete for the same herds, so a City that drinks more leaves less to be made into cheese, and everyone who eats cheese pays for it. A model with one curve per product and no transformation tying them together could not have produced that at all: it is the dual use of milk, and nothing else, that carries the effect across.

Cheese also rises by much less, in percentage terms, than milk does — 4.2% against 5.6%. A good part of what a tonne of cheese costs is the making, and a thirstier City does not make a cheesemaker dearer.

Behind the prices, the quantities tell the same story twice: the City drinks 602 tonnes of milk before and 711 after, while the dairy's output falls from 150 tonnes of cheese to 147. The extra glasses came out of the cheese.
