Two regions and a dairy

The smallest model that is still a market: two regions, two products, one transformation and one trade route. Everything else in this package is more of these same four things.

The Pasture has the cows; the City has the people. Milk is either drunk as it is or turned into cheese, in either place, and either milk or cheese can be carried between the two at a cost. Four questions follow, and none of them has an obvious answer: how much of the milk is drunk and how much goes to the dairy, where the dairy stands, what each region pays, and what travels. Nobody decides. The answer is whatever clears both markets at once.

using Markets, DataFrames

The economy

Two regions and two products. Quantities are tonnes, prices euros per tonne.

regions  = ["Pasture", "City"]
products = ["milk", "cheese"]

Milk comes from the herds, on a curve through one observed point: last year the Pasture sold 2000 at €400 the tonne. The City keeps a few herds at its edges, where land is dear.

supply = [SupplySpec(product = "milk", region = "Pasture", p0 = 400, q0 = 2000, elasticity = 0.6),
          SupplySpec(product = "milk", region = "City",    p0 = 450, q0 =  300, elasticity = 0.6)]

Now the part that makes this more than an arithmetic exercise: milk is both a final product and an input. People drink it, and dairies turn it into cheese. Each use has its own demand, and neither is told how much milk it may have.

demand = [DemandSpec(product = "milk",   region = "Pasture", p0 =  400, q0 = 200, elasticity = 0.3),
          DemandSpec(product = "milk",   region = "City",    p0 =  450, q0 = 600, elasticity = 0.3),
          DemandSpec(product = "cheese", region = "Pasture", p0 = 5300, q0 =  30, elasticity = 0.5),
          DemandSpec(product = "cheese", region = "City",    p0 = 5350, q0 = 120, elasticity = 0.5)]

One transformation, and it may run in either region. Cheesemaking is a fixed recipe — ten tonnes of milk to the tonne of cheese, the rest leaving as whey and water — and €1300 covers everything the model does not name: the rennet, the cellar, the cheesemaker.

processes = [Process(name    = "dairy",
                     inputs  = [leontief(product = "milk", coeff = 10.0)],
                     outputs = ["cheese" => 1.0],
                     vacost  = 1300)]

Both products can be carried either way, at €45 the tonne. Writing a route down is what makes a product tradable — anything with no route stays where it was made — and routes are directional, hence four entries for two products. Note that the rate is the same for both: nothing in the data says that one is cheaper to move than the other.

transport_costs = Dict(("milk",   "Pasture", "City") => 45.0, ("milk",   "City", "Pasture") => 45.0,
                       ("cheese", "Pasture", "City") => 45.0, ("cheese", "City", "Pasture") => 45.0)

Looking before solving

The pieces go into one MarketData. Typing its name gives back only how much it holds; printing it gives a summary of the economy that was actually written down: what each product does, how the processes chain, where goods may go and at what cost. It is the cheapest check there is that the data say what was meant.

economy = MarketData(; regions, products, demand, supply, processes, transport_costs)
MarketData(2 regions, 2 products, 1 process)

The mathematical program it becomes can be read too, before anything is solved: jump_model returns the JuMP model that solve_market would hand to the solver, with every variable and constraint named after the region and product it belongs to. Here it is one material balance per region and product, whose duals will be the prices, and an objective that is the net social surplus.

Solving

result  = solve_market(economy)

What comes out

Prices first. They are not assumptions: they are the shadow values of the material balances — what one more tonne of each product would be worth in each place.

Three things in those tables were never put in.

The milk splits itself. Of the 2301 tonnes the herds give, 801 are drunk and 1500 go to the dairy. Nothing in the data said how to divide them: the two uses bid against each other for the same milk, and the price that settles the contest is the one that makes every market balance at once.

The City makes no cheese at all — its entry in the production table is missing, meaning the dairy never runs there. Bringing in a tonne of cheese costs €45 of freight; bringing in the ten tonnes of milk behind it costs €450. So every cheese eaten in the City was made in the Pasture, and the model was not told that, it worked it out.

Milk still crosses, but only the milk that is drunk. The City's own herds cover part of its thirst and the rest is carried in. Not one tonne of milk is carried in order to be made into cheese.

Two things worth checking by hand

A model is worth no more than the conditions it satisfies, and in one this small each can be checked on a single line.

The dairy earns no profit. Where it runs, the cheese price must cover exactly ten tonnes of milk plus the €1300 — any more and another dairy would open, any less and this one would shut:

price(r, p) = only(result.prices[(result.prices.region .== r) .&
                                 (result.prices.product .== p), :price])
(cheese = price("Pasture", "cheese"), milk_plus_making = 10 * price("Pasture", "milk") + 1300)
(cheese = 5308.545270473618, milk_plus_making = 5308.545270474511)

Carrying earns no profit either. On a route that is used, the price gap between its two ends is exactly the freight — any wider and somebody would ship more until it closed. Both routes carry something here, so both gaps are €45:

(milk   = price("City", "milk")   - price("Pasture", "milk"),
 cheese = price("City", "cheese") - price("Pasture", "cheese"))
(milk = 44.99999999955929, cheese = 44.99999999888405)

Asking it something

The tables above are not the point — you could have measured those. The point is the question you cannot measure: what if something changed?

Suppose the City takes to drinking milk, and wants a fifth more of it at every price. That is a shifter on the demand curve; the observed point the curve was fitted to stays exactly where it was.

thirsty = [d.product == "milk" && d.region == "City" ?
               DemandSpec(product = "milk", region = "City", p0 = d.p0, q0 = d.q0,
                          elasticity = d.elasticity, shift = 1.2) : d
           for d in demand]
thirst = solve_market(MarketData(; regions, products, demand = thirsty, supply,
                                   processes, transport_costs))

after(r, p) = only(thirst.prices[(thirst.prices.region .== r) .&
                                 (thirst.prices.product .== p), :price])
DataFrame((region = r, product = p, before = round(price(r, p); digits = 1),
           after  = round(after(r, p); digits = 1),
           change_pct = round(100 * (after(r, p) / price(r, p) - 1); digits = 1))
          for r in regions for p in products)

Nobody's taste for cheese changed, and yet cheese is dearer — in the Pasture as much as in the City. The two uses of milk compete for the same herds, so a City that drinks more leaves less to be made into cheese, and everyone who eats cheese pays for it. A model with one curve per product and no transformation tying them together could not have produced that at all: it is the dual use of milk, and nothing else, that carries the effect across.

Cheese also rises by much less, in percentage terms, than milk does — 4.2% against 5.6%. A good part of what a tonne of cheese costs is the making, and a thirstier City does not make a cheesemaker dearer.

Behind the prices, the quantities tell the same story twice: the City drinks 602 tonnes of milk before and 711 after, while the dairy's output falls from 150 tonnes of cheese to 147. The extra glasses came out of the cheese.
