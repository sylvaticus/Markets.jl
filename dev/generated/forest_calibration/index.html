The forest sector: calibrating with partial data

The first page wrote the model with the numbers at hand before any calibration effort, and its reference run landed some way from them. This page builds the same model — the same regions, products, mills and elasticities — from the statistics a real application would actually have: incomplete, rounded, and not quite consistent with each other. The aim is a reference run that sits on the data, as near as the data allow.

The general ideas are in Calibration and Calibrating with partial data; this page is the worked case.

For illustrative purposes only

The data are plausible, but they are not statistics. They are rounded the way published figures are, small flows are missing as they are from trade statistics, and they do not balance exactly — like real data.

using Markets, DataFrames, DelimitedFiles

france  = ["SEF", "SWF", "GEF", "OF"]
blocs   = ["EU", "NA", "AS", "RW"]
regions = [france; blocs]

            "sawn_sw", "sawn_hw", "panel", "plywood", "paper", "pellets"]

Three kinds of numbers

Everything the model needs falls into one of three groups, and keeping them apart is most of the discipline of calibration.

  1. Observed: what happened in the base year — what each region produced, used and consumed, at what prices, what crossed borders, what plant there was. It comes from statistics, and is read from files below.
  2. Chosen: how the economy responds, which a single year cannot show — elasticities of supply and demand, and how easily one product or one origin replaces another. They come from the literature or from your own estimation, and are written in this script, where they can be argued about.
  3. Calibrated: everything else — the reference points of the curves, the shares of every CES, the costs of the mills, and here also the flows the statistics do not report. These are never guessed: they are whatever makes the observed year an equilibrium of the chosen behaviour.

The statistics at hand

The data are long-format CSV files, one observation per row, the shape statistics come in. DelimitedFiles, from the standard library, is enough to read them; in a real application you would more likely reach for CSV.jl.

data = pkgdir(Markets, "examples", "forest", "data")

function read_table(file)
    cells, header = readdlm(joinpath(data, file), ',', header = true)
    text(c) = c isa AbstractString ? String(c) : c
    return DataFrame([String(h) => identity.(text.(cells[:, j]))
                      for (j, h) in enumerate(vec(header))])
end

# a column of a table as a dictionary, keyed by the columns named
lookup(table, value, keys...) =
    Dict(Tuple(row[k] for k in keys) => row[value] for row in eachrow(table))

From statistics to model

The flows nobody reported

Calibrating trade needs a full matrix of flows between regions, and the statistics give part of it. What they give is enough to fill in the rest:

  • between the blocs, the flows themselves, from customs;
  • what each bloc keeps of its own production — production less exports;
  • and for the French corner of the matrix, its edges: what each French region produced and used, and what France as a whole bought from and sold to each bloc.

The corner itself — which French region shipped how much to which, and which of them the trade with each bloc went through — is filled in by impute_flows: the flows closest to a gravity pattern that add up to all those edges. In that small table the French regions stand for themselves, and each bloc stands for its trade with France: what it sent into France, as an origin, and what it took from France, as a destination. Blocs do not trade with each other in it — that trade is already known.

customs_flow = lookup(customs, :quantity, :product, :from, :to)
from_customs(p, o, r) = get(customs_flow, (p, o, r), 0.0)   # unreported: under a thousand

function french_corner(p)
    into_france = Dict(b => from_customs(p, b, "France") for b in blocs)
    out_of_france = Dict(b => from_customs(p, "France", b) for b in blocs)
    return impute_flows(
        production = merge(Dict(r => made[(r, p)] for r in france if haskey(made, (r, p))),
                           into_france),
        use        = merge(Dict(r => used[(r, p)] for r in france if haskey(used, (r, p))),
                           out_of_france),
        distance = distance, decay = 1.4, home_bias = 3.0,
        totals = Dict((blocs, blocs) => 0.0))
end

The gravity parameters say how fast trade falls off with distance and how much a region leans toward itself. They are guesses, and need not be good ones: what the imputed flows must get right is their totals, which they do by construction. How little the guess matters is measured in Calibrating with partial data.

With the corner filled in, the whole matrix:

flows = Dict{Tuple{String,String,String},Float64}()
for p in products
    for ((o, r), x) in french_corner(p)
        x > 0 && (flows[(p, o, r)] = x)
    end
    for o in blocs, r in blocs
        o != r && from_customs(p, o, r) > 0 && (flows[(p, o, r)] = from_customs(p, o, r))
    end
    for b in blocs
        haskey(made, (b, p)) || continue
        exported = sum(from_customs(p, b, r) for r in [blocs; "France"] if r != b)
        flows[(p, b, b)] = made[(b, p)] - exported
    end
end

Two prices per region

A variety costs its buyer what its producer was paid, plus the duty if there is one, plus the freight to get it there:

πˢ = lookup(producer_prices, :price, :region, :product)   # producer prices
delivered(p, o, r) = o == r ? πˢ[(o, p)] :
                     πˢ[(o, p)] * (1 + duty(p, o)) + transport_costs[(p, o, r)]

Each region buys a mix of varieties at different delivered prices, so two prices coexist in every region: what its producers get, from the statistics, and what its users pay for the mix. The second is the average delivered price of what the region bought,

\[\pi^c_r = \frac{\sum_o X_{o,r}\, q_{o,r}}{\sum_o X_{o,r}},\]

the value of its purchases divided by their volume — which is how a statistical office computes a consumer unit value, and also the price the calibrated model charges for the mix when it reproduces the base year.

πᶜ = Dict{Tuple{String,String},Float64}()
for r in regions, p in products
    bought = [(o, x) for ((q, o, d), x) in flows if q == p && d == r && x > 0]
    isempty(bought) && continue
    πᶜ[(r, p)] = sum(x * delivered(p, o, r) for (o, x) in bought) / sum(last, bought)
end

Trade shares

A destination's composite is a CES over its origins, and its shares are not the observed shares: they are the shares each origin would have if all delivered prices were equal. armington_calibration inverts the CES demand condition through every level of the origin tree, from the flows and the delivered prices. It also sets the scale of each composite, so that the composite counts the m³ the flows bring in: the m³ the flows bring in are then the m³ the mills and the households take out.

armington = [armington_calibration(
                 product = p, sigma = σ_blocs[p], nests = [europe(p)],
                 flows   = Dict((o, r) => x for ((q, o, r), x) in flows if q == p),
                 prices  = Dict((o, r) => delivered(p, o, r)
                                for o in regions, r in regions if haskey(πˢ, (o, p))))
             for p in products]

A route that carried nothing in the base year, or too little to be reported, gets no share, and stays closed whatever prices do: the well-known limit of Armington calibration.

Demand

Each bundle is calibrated as on the first page, with ces_calibration, but on each region's consumer prices and consumption rather than on one price for all: the household in OF buys the mix of French and imported pellets, at what that mix costs in OF.

consumed = lookup(consumption, :quantity, :region, :product)

function construction_demand(r)
    inner = ces_calibration(quantities = [consumed[(r, p)] for p in lumber],
                            prices     = [πᶜ[(r, p)] for p in lumber], sigma = σ_lumber)
    outer = ces_calibration(quantities = [consumed[(r, "sawn_hw")], inner.quantity],
                            prices     = [πᶜ[(r, "sawn_hw")], inner.price],
                            sigma = σ_construction)
    return DemandCurve(region = r, p0 = outer.price, q0 = outer.quantity,
        elasticity = η_construction,
        product = DemandNest(sigma = σ_construction,
            products = ["sawn_hw"], shares = [outer.shares[1]],
            nests = [DemandNest(sigma = σ_lumber, share = outer.shares[2],
                                products = lumber, shares = inner.shares)]))
end

function fuel_demand(r, gas = gas_reference)
    fuel = ces_calibration(quantities = [consumed[(r, p)] for p in fuels],
                           prices     = [πᶜ[(r, p)] for p in fuels], sigma = σ_fuel)
    return DemandCurve(region = r, p0 = fuel.price, q0 = fuel.quantity, elasticity = η_fuel,
        shift = exogenous_shift(gas = (level = gas, reference = gas_reference,
                                       elasticity = gas_cross_elasticity)),
        product = DemandNest(sigma = σ_fuel, products = fuels, shares = fuel.shares))
end

build_demand(gas = gas_reference) =
    [[construction_demand(r) for r in regions];
     [fuel_demand(r, gas) for r in regions];
     [DemandCurve(product = "paper", region = r, p0 = πᶜ[(r, "paper")],
                 q0 = consumed[(r, "paper")], elasticity = η_paper) for r in regions]]

demand = build_demand()

The mills

A mill that can choose its wood is calibrated like a demand bundle, from the observed mix at the consumer prices of the region it was observed in. Its quantities are all m³ of wood, so here they do add up, and the scale of the calibration lets the bundle keep counting m³: the composite is then the m³ of wood a unit of output takes, as the conversion factor says it.

function bundle(process)
    mix = input_mix[input_mix.process .== process, :]
    c = ces_calibration(quantities = mix.quantity,
                        prices = [πᶜ[(r, p)] for (r, p) in zip(mix.region, mix.product)],
                        sigma = σ_mills[process])
    return ces(composite = sum(mix.quantity), products = mix.product, shares = c.shares,
               sigma = σ_mills[process], phi = c.scale)
end

The shares are the same wherever the process runs, so a pellet mill in SWF, facing other prices, uses another mix — the one those prices make cheapest. That is why one region's mix was enough.

The vacost of a mill — labour, energy, capital — is never observed in the units the model wants, but the base year implies it. A mill that ran and did not expand earned no more than its costs, so its cost is its margin: what its outputs fetched at the producer prices of its region, less what its inputs cost at the consumer prices there. For a bundle, the cost of the inputs is its price index at the region's prices, per m³ of the bundle.

price_index(δ, p, σ) = sum(δ .* p .^ (1 - σ))^(1 / (1 - σ))
input_cost(n::Nest, r) = length(n.products) == 1 ?
    n.composite * πᶜ[(r, only(n.products))] :
    n.composite * price_index(n.shares, [πᶜ[(r, p)] for p in n.products], n.sigma) / n.phi
margin(inputs, outputs, r) = sum(y * πˢ[(r, k)] for (k, y) in outputs) -
                             sum(input_cost(n, r) for n in inputs)
operating(outputs) = [r for r in regions if get(made, (r, first(first(outputs))), 0.0) > 0]

The margins differ from region to region, as wages and energy do, which is why a process takes a region => cost dictionary. They are positive everywhere, the second check a base year has to pass: a negative one would say a mill ran at a loss, and the model would close it. A mill running flat out could have earned a rent on top of its costs, which one year cannot tell apart from them; that is why the capacities in the data are those of plants with room to spare. Each becomes a limit on the activity of its plants:

plant_limits = [@limit("$(row.process) plant $(row.region)",
                       activity(region = row.region, process = row.process) <= row.capacity)
                for row in eachrow(capacities)]

processes = [Process(name = name, inputs = inputs, outputs = outputs,
                     regions  = operating(outputs),
                     vacost   = Dict(r => margin(inputs, outputs, r) for r in operating(outputs)))
             for (name, inputs, outputs) in technology]

How close does it land to the data?

Every curve, share and cost was fitted to one observation, and each passes through it exactly. But the observations do not quite agree with each other, and an equilibrium has to satisfy every balance at once, so it settles near every observation and on none. How near is the first thing to look at, every time. Model trade is compared at the level customs report it, with the French regions added up:

results_dir = joinpath(pkgdir(Markets), "examples", "forest", "results")

function outcome(result, scenario)
    t = outerjoin(select(result.prices, :region, :product, :price, :producer_price),
                  rename(result.production, :quantity => :production),
                  select(result.consumption, :region, :product, :quantity => :consumption),
                  on = [:region, :product])
    return insertcols!(t, 1, :scenario => scenario)
end

function save_outcomes(file, runs)
    t = vcat((outcome(r, s) for (s, r) in runs)...)
    cell(x) = ismissing(x) ? "" : x isa Real ? string(round(x; sigdigits = 6)) : string(x)
    mkpath(results_dir)
    open(joinpath(results_dir, file), "w") do io
        println(io, join(names(t), ","))
        foreach(row -> println(io, join(cell.(collect(row)), ",")), eachrow(t))
    end
end

What the wood is made of

The model counts m³ and tonnes of products. What those products are made of — the carbon in them, say — can be traced through the same solution with material_accounts, which neither needs nor changes anything in the equilibrium; see Material accounts.

A Material needs three things. Its content per unit of primary supply: about a quarter of a tonne of carbon in a m³ of wood, a little more in hardwood, which is denser. What each mill loses of what enters it: the sawdust and bark a sawmill does not turn into lumber or chips, the half of the wood a pulp mill burns for energy. And, for a mill with several outputs, how it divides the rest; by default every unit of output gets the same content, which suits a sawmill whose lumber and chips are both m³ of the same wood. The figures are illustrative.

carbon = Material(name = "carbon",
                  content = Dict("swr" => 0.23, "hwr" => 0.29, "smallwood" => 0.25),
                  losses  = Dict("sawmill_sw" => 0.15, "sawmill_hw" => 0.20,
                                 "plymill" => 0.10, "panelmill" => 0.05,
                                 "pulpmill" => 0.50, "papermill" => 0.05,
                                 "pelletmill" => 0.10))
accounts = material_accounts(res, carbon)

The accounts close for every region and product, and for the world: the carbon harvested is the carbon in what was consumed, plus what the mills lost on the way, in million tonnes:
